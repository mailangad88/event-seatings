When someone dies, the house of mourning becomes both private and public: close family need quiet, and a stream of visitors arrives with food and condolences. The pain point is that no one has capacity for logistics. Chairs, meals, a schedule for prayers, and basic supplies all need handling. This guide is for the family and for friends who want to help, and it offers practical choices that you can adapt to your own customs.

## What shiva is, and how practice varies

Shiva (meaning "seven") is a mourning period that begins after the burial for the close relatives of the deceased: parents, children, siblings and spouses. Mourners stay at home, often at the home of the deceased or a family member, and the community comes to them. Visitors bring food, offer comfort and often join prayers. Many Orthodox and Conservative families observe the full seven days (with a break on Shabbat), others observe three days or one, and some Reform and secular families adapt the practice. Some cover mirrors, wear a torn ribbon (kriah), sit on low stools or chairs, and light a memorial candle that burns for the seven days. Some families do not do these. Every family and community differs, so follow your rabbi and your own comfort.

A meal of consolation (seudat havra'ah) is traditionally prepared by friends and neighbors when the mourners return from the cemetery, and mourners do not typically cook or host during shiva. Visitors typically do not greet the mourners, wait for the mourner to speak first, and avoid small talk.

## What to set up

**Seating.** Provide enough chairs for visitors, as shiva visits can bring a crowd. If your family uses low chairs or stools for mourners, set those out. Folding chairs can be borrowed or rented, and a rental company can often deliver within a day. Check [comfortable chairs for older guests](/blog/comfortable-chairs-for-older-guests) for options.

**Memorial candle.** A seven-day candle is lit at the start of shiva and kept in a safe place on a non-flammable surface.

**Prayer needs.** If prayers are held, you may need prayer books (siddurim), kippot (head coverings) for visitors, and a quiet place for a minyan, which in traditional communities requires ten adults. The synagogue often supplies books and helps organize.

**Food table.** A large table for dishes, a coffee station, water pitchers, plates, cups and napkins. Many families have so much food that they need a second table or a refrigerator plan.

**Entry.** A mat for shoes, a coat rack, a guest book and a plan for parking.

**Supplies.** Paper goods, trash bags, extra toilet paper and tissues. Disposable plates and cups reduce cleanup, and many Jewish homes that keep kosher need to keep dishes separate, so ask.

Many friends use a shared sign-up sheet or a meal calendar so food is evenly spread, so the family isn't overwhelmed one day and starving the next. Ask about kosher rules, allergies and a no-meat or dairy preference.

## Etiquette for visitors

Arrive during posted hours, stay briefly, and let the mourner lead the conversation. Bring food that is kosher if the family keeps kosher, ideally in disposable containers. Avoid flowers in traditional homes, as many families prefer donations or food. Dress modestly. Turn off your phone. Share a memory of the person. A note or a donation to charity in the person's memory is a customary gesture. After shiva, keep checking in, since grief continues long after the visitors leave.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is shiva?

Shiva is the Jewish mourning period, traditionally seven days after burial, when close relatives stay at home and the community visits to comfort them. Practices vary by community and family.

### How many chairs do I need for shiva?

Plan seats for 15 to 30 visitors on busy days, and more for an evening with a minyan. Chairs can be borrowed from neighbors or the synagogue, or rented for delivery.

### What food is served during shiva?

Visitors bring dishes, often dairy or pareve, and the first meal is the meal of consolation, which commonly includes eggs and round foods such as lentils or bagels. Follow kosher customs if your family keeps them.

### Who prepares the meals for shiva?

Friends, neighbors and the community traditionally provide meals, so the mourners do not need to cook. A coordinator can manage a shared calendar.

### Do I need a minyan during shiva?

In traditional communities, prayers with mourners' Kaddish require a minyan of ten adults. Your synagogue can help organize one. Reform and some other families may not require it.

### What should I bring to a shiva visit?

Food that follows the family's dietary rules, or a donation in the person's memory. Many families prefer food to flowers. Ask the family first if unsure.

### How long should a shiva visit last?

Usually 15 to 30 minutes. Let the mourners lead, and leave when others arrive or when the family seems tired.

### Can shiva be shorter than seven days?

Yes. Some families observe three days or fewer. Ask your rabbi what is right for your family.
