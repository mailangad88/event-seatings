**Quick answer**

Set up registration with one check-in station per 50 to 75 arriving guests per hour, split lines by last name, and keep a separate table for walk-ups and problems. Print badges or wristbands ahead, use a clean guest list, and place the area away from the doors so lines don't block traffic. Open 30 to 45 minutes before the start and staff at least two people per station.

</div>

Check-in is the first thing guests experience, and the first place an event can go wrong. A long line in a doorway, a missing name on the list or a badge printer that jams can set a bad tone before the program starts. A clear setup, enough staff and a plan for exceptions turns check-in into a quick welcome instead of a wait.

Speeds vary with the method. Scanning QR codes is faster than looking names up, and prepaid, pre-printed badges are faster than collecting payment. Test your process with a few colleagues before the day.

## Choose your check-in method

The simplest method is a printed alphabetical list and pre-printed name tags. It needs no devices and works anywhere, but it is slow if the list is long and you must update it by hand. Tablets or laptops with registration software let you search, scan QR codes and mark attendance in real time, though they need power and a reliable connection. Many events use both: scanning for speed and a printed list as backup.

If you need badges printed on site, add a printer station and test it. For events with tickets or payment, plan a separate line for payment issues so they don't slow everyone else. For a clear registration process before the event, see [how to increase event attendance](/blog/how-to-increase-event-attendance).

## Layout and flow

Place registration just inside the entrance but not in the doorway, with room for a queue to form without blocking the way. A straight or snaking line works with stanchions or tape. Put signs above head height with letter ranges, and put a greeter at the front to direct people.

Behind or beside the check-in tables, place badge supplies, a table for handouts or swag, and a space for staff to store bags. Keep the path to the main room clear. Ask your venue about electrical outlets and Wi-Fi coverage near the entrance. Our [ADA accessible seating and pathways guide](/blog/ada-accessible-event-seating-and-pathways) lists clearances for wheelchair users and a lowered table if needed.

## Staffing and exceptions

Brief staff on the process, the top five questions and who to call for problems. Give each station a printed list, a pen and clear instructions for last-minute walk-ups, name changes and missing badges. Have a manager or lead float to solve issues.

Plan for dietary and accessibility needs. If guests have meal choices or special seating, the check-in should confirm them and hand out meal cards or table assignments. See [dietary restrictions planning](/blog/dietary-restrictions-planning-for-events). For volunteer-run check-in, review [volunteer coordination](/blog/volunteer-coordination-for-nonprofit-events) for roles and training.

## Check-in setup steps

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Clean the guest list.</strong> Merge duplicates, add meal and access notes, and print copies.</li>
<li><strong>Choose a method.</strong> Scanning, search or printed list, with a backup.</li>
<li><strong>Prepare badges.</strong> Pre-print names and sort them alphabetically.</li>
<li><strong>Plan the layout.</strong> Tables, signs, queue and a help desk.</li>
<li><strong>Brief staff.</strong> Practice the process and the top exceptions.</li>
<li><strong>Open early.</strong> Start 30 to 45 minutes before the program.</li>
<li><strong>Reconcile after.</strong> Mark no-shows and walk-ups for next time.</li>
</ol>

## Chicago-area notes

Chicago weather affects check-in. In winter, guests arrive with coats, snow and wet boots, so keep the registration area warm and near a coat check, with mats for the floor. In summer, outdoor check-in needs shade, so a tent or canopy over the tables protects staff and paperwork from sun and rain. For downtown buildings, security desks may require a guest list sent in advance, so ask the building. Suburban venues may have a single entrance, so plan the queue carefully. Public parking and transit can mean staggered arrivals, so open check-in early.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I set up event registration?

Clean your guest list, choose a check-in method, prepare badges, set up tables and a queue, brief staff and open early. Add a separate help desk for walk-ups and problems.

### How many check-in stations do I need?

A common planning estimate is one station per 50 to 75 guests arriving in the same hour. Scanning can be faster, and staggered arrivals need fewer.

### How early should check-in open?

Thirty to forty-five minutes before the start time is typical. Open earlier for large events or those with security screening.

### Should I use paper lists or software?

Software is faster for large events and updates in real time. A printed list is a valuable backup in case of connectivity issues.

### How many staff do I need at registration?

Plan two people per station and a lead to handle problems. Larger events need a greeter and a person for badges.

### What is the best way to handle walk-ups?

Use a separate table so they don't slow the main lines. Have forms, a payment method if needed, and badges ready.

### Where should registration be located?

Just inside the entrance, with room for a line that doesn't block the door, and away from the main program room so noise doesn't carry.
