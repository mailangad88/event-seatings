An aisle runner is a long strip of fabric or paper that covers the walkway to the ceremony. It protects the bride's dress on grass, hides a worn carpet, and points every guest's eyes toward the couple. Choosing one is mostly arithmetic and a few practical questions about the ground, the weather and the venue.

## How long should an aisle runner be?

Measure from where the bride starts walking to where the couple will stand, then add **3 to 5 feet** so the runner can be tucked, trimmed or overlapped. A 40-foot aisle needs a runner of roughly 45 to 46 feet. Common widths are 3 to 4 feet, which is wide enough for a bride with a full skirt and an escort. Ask your rental company which lengths it stocks, because a runner that is too short looks worse than none.

| Aisle | Typical runner length | Common scenario |
|---|---|---|
| 20 ft | 25 ft | Small backyard or living room |
| 30 ft | 35 ft | Garden or ballroom |
| 40 ft | 45 to 46 ft | Banquet hall or church nave |
| 60 ft | 65 ft | Long lawn or cathedral |

## Fabric choices

- **Cloth (satin, linen, burlap):** the best look and the most durable. Satin is slick, so tape or stake it well.
- **Paper or poly:** lower cost, light, easy to dispose of. It can tear in wind.
- **Rolled fabric with printed patterns or petals:** a decorative choice for boho and garden themes.
- **Carpet-style:** thicker and safer on grass, but heavier to move.

White and ivory are classic. Colors should contrast with the floor, not blend into it.

## Staking, taping and weights

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Indoors on carpet:</strong> use rug tape or weighted corners and ask the venue before you stick anything down.</li>
<li><strong>On hardwood or marble:</strong> use removable, venue-approved tape or a non-slip backing.</li>
<li><strong>On grass:</strong> use landscape staples or pins, and check for sprinklers and underground lines. Illinois requires a JULIE call (811) before digging, and stakes in park district land need permission.</li>
<li><strong>Outdoors in wind:</strong> pick a heavier runner and anchor both edges every few feet.</li>
<li><strong>Rain plan:</strong> a wet satin runner is slippery. Have a plan B or a textured runner.</li>
</ol>

## Who unrolls it and when?

Traditionally an usher or the ring bearer unrolls it just before the processional. Some couples keep it down from the start. A runner that is already down can get stepped on by early guests, so use it right before the entrance if it matters. Rehearse once. A runner that catches on a heel at the top of the aisle is a bad memory.

## Chicago details

Chicago weather changes the runner choice. In April and October, a cold, wet lawn makes paper limp. In July, black asphalt or a hot driveway can scorch thin fabric. Church aisles often have a long, narrow nave with fixed pews, so measure from the church door to the chancel, and ask the church what is allowed. Some churches limit tape or petals.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Measuring the aisle but not the entrance.
- Choosing a runner narrower than a wedding dress train.
- Taping to a floor without venue approval.
- Using a slick runner on a slope.


## Frequently asked questions

### How long should an aisle runner be?

Measure the walk to the altar and add 3 to 5 feet. A 40-foot aisle needs about 45 to 46 feet. Confirm the stocked lengths with the rental company.

### How wide should an aisle runner be?

Three to four feet is common, enough for a bride with a full skirt and an escort. Go wider for a grand procession.

### What is the best aisle runner for grass?

A heavier cloth or carpet-style runner staked at the edges. Paper and slick satin move in wind and on slopes.

### Can you put an aisle runner on carpet?

Yes. Use rug tape or weighted corners and check with the venue before using adhesives.

### Who rolls out the aisle runner?

Usually an usher or ring bearer right before the processional. Rehearse it once so it lies flat.

### Do I need an aisle runner at all?

No. It helps on grass, worn floors or in a long aisle, but is optional. Petals, lanterns or markers can also frame the aisle.
