**Quick answer**

Tell guests how to arrive before they ask. Share the exact address, the best parking option, a backup lot or street plan, the nearest train or bus stop, a rideshare drop-off point and an accessible entrance. Send the details with the invitation and again a few days before. Plan separately for vendors, who need a loading spot. Clear directions reduce late arrivals and stress.

In Chicago, getting to an event can be harder than the event itself. City guests face meters, permit zones and construction. Suburban guests face lots that fill up or are across a busy road. Older guests may not manage a long walk. A simple parking and transit plan protects your start time and your guests' mood.

## Who needs what

Different people need different information. Local guests want the fastest route and parking choices. Out-of-town guests need hotel, airport and transit tips. Older guests and people with mobility needs need a drop-off close to the door and a step-free path; see [ADA accessible event seating and pathways](/blog/ada-accessible-event-seating-and-pathways). Vendors need load-in directions and a spot to unload; see [Chicago event delivery parking and loading zones](/blog/chicago-event-delivery-parking-loading-zones) and [alley deliveries](/blog/chicago-alley-deliveries-event-rentals).

A good plan covers five parts: the address with a landmark, the parking choice, the backup, the transit option and the drop-off. Write each in a sentence or two, so the guest does not have to guess.

## Choosing a parking approach

Which option fits depends on guest count and location. These are general tendencies, not rules.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Situation | Good approach | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Under 40 guests, residential street | Street parking and a neighbor heads-up | Permit zones, snow rules, blocked driveways |
| 40 to 100 guests, city venue | Nearby lot or garage plus transit | Garage hours, event pricing, flood of arrivals |
| 100+ guests, suburban venue | On-site lot, shuttle or valet | Lot capacity, overflow, traffic at the exit |
| Evening event, alcohol served | Rideshare and transit encouraged | Late-night pickup surge and cold waits |
| Winter event | Close parking and indoor wait space | Ice, snow removal, long walks in coats |

</div>

Confirm lot capacity and any event fees directly with the venue or lot operator.

## Build your plan in six steps

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Estimate cars.</strong> Divide guests by two or three to estimate vehicles.</li>
<li><strong>Check the venue.</strong> Ask about onsite parking, overflow, valet and loading access.</li>
<li><strong>Scout the route.</strong> Drive or walk it at the same time of day, noting meters, construction and turns.</li>
<li><strong>Choose a drop-off.</strong> Pick a spot for rideshare and accessible drop-off, and name it.</li>
<li><strong>Write simple directions.</strong> Short sentences, landmarks, no jargon.</li>
<li><strong>Send twice.</strong> With the invitation and about a week before the event.</li>
</ol>

## Valet, shuttles and rideshare

Valet works well for formal events but needs space, staff and insurance questions; ask the venue or a licensed provider. Shuttles can help when parking is limited, such as for a rural or lakefront venue. For events with alcohol, encourage rideshare, taxis or designated drivers and make it easy by naming the pickup spot. Remember vendors too: share the load-in plan from [event rental delivery day: what to expect](/blog/event-rental-delivery-day-what-to-expect).

## What to do on the day

Station a greeter or volunteer at the parking entrance or drop-off to point guests in the right direction. Put a sign at the turn that matches the photo or landmark in your directions. Keep your phone on in case a guest cannot find the entrance, and name one person to answer parking questions. If something changes, such as a closed lot, send a short update message right away so guests can adapt.

## Chicago-area notes

For Chicago city events, check street parking rules, permit zones and meter hours, and consider that some neighborhoods have limited weekend parking. CTA trains and buses can be the easiest option downtown, and Metra works for many suburban guests, but check schedules for late nights and weekends. Winter parking can be tough because of snow routes and reduced street space. For lakefront and park events, see [Chicago Park District event permit](/blog/chicago-park-district-event-permit). Suburban venues usually have lots, but large events may need overflow plans or shuttles. When in doubt, ask your village or the venue what to expect.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I tell guests where to park?

Include the address, the best parking option, any cost, a backup, and a short walking description. Add a map link and repeat the info in a reminder a week before.

### How many parking spaces will I need?

A common planning guess is one space for every two to three guests. Couples, families and carpools change the number. Ask the venue about capacity.

### Should I hire valet for my event?

Valet suits formal events and tight sites, but it needs a staging area and a trained provider. Ask your venue about requirements.

### What if there is not enough parking?

Consider a shuttle, an overflow lot, rideshare encouragement or a different start time. Tell guests early so they can plan.

### How do I plan for guests with limited mobility?

Provide an accessible drop-off close to the entrance and a step-free route. Ask your venue about accessible parking and doors.

### Where should rideshare drop off?

Pick a safe, visible spot near the entrance and describe it in your directions. Avoid blocking driveways or fire lanes.

### What about vendor parking?

Give vendors a separate loading spot and a time window. Ask the venue about dock access and permits.
