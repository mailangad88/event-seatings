**Quick answer**

Use cords marked for outdoor use, with a heavy enough gauge for the load and length: 12 gauge for long runs or heavy loads, 14 gauge for moderate ones, and 16 gauge only for short, light runs. Plug into a GFCI-protected outlet, keep connections off wet ground in a weatherproof cover, never daisy-chain more than you must, and never run a cord under a rug or through a doorway that closes on it.

</div>

At most outdoor events the cords are the part nobody plans. The caterer needs one outlet. The DJ needs two. The string lights are 80 feet from the nearest plug, and someone grabs the orange cord from the garage. Most cord problems are not dramatic. They are tripped guests, tripped breakers, cords that run warm, and connections that sit in a wet patch of grass. All can be prevented with a few simple choices.

## Pick the right cord

Look at the label. Outdoor cords are marked with a W or "suitable for outdoor use" and list the gauge, the length and the amp or watt rating.

The reason is voltage drop. A thin cord over a long distance wastes power as heat and starves the device at the end. That is how a coffee urn takes forever to heat or a speaker pops.

## Know your load

Add the watts of everything on one cord and divide by volts to find amps. For a 120-volt supply, a 1,500-watt warmer draws about 12.5 amps. That is a heavy load that needs a 12-gauge cord and its own circuit. The [power needs checklist](/blog/event-power-needs-checklist) shows how to list loads by vendor. If you are on a generator, see [generator sizing](/blog/generator-size-for-events).

## GFCI: not optional outside

A ground fault circuit interrupter shuts off power when current leaks, for example into a wet lawn or a person. Outdoor outlets should be GFCI protected. If the outlet is not, use a portable GFCI adapter or a GFCI power strip rated for outdoor use. Press the test and reset buttons before the event. A GFCI that trips often is warning you about moisture or a damaged cord. Do not bypass it.

## Connections: the weak point

Plug connections are where water gets in.

- **Raise them.** Put connections on a bench, a stake or an upside-down bucket, off the ground.
- **Cover them.** Use a weatherproof connection cover, which is a small plastic clamshell box built for cord ends.
- **Add a drip loop.** Let the cord sag below the plug so water runs off.
- **Do not tape plugs together.** Electrical tape is not a seal.
- **Avoid daisy-chains.** Chaining several cords adds resistance and extra weak points. One long cord of the right gauge is better.

## Protect the cords

- Do not run cords under rugs, tarps or tent flooring that can trap heat.
- Do not run cords through doors, windows or gates that close on them.
- Do not use cords with cracked insulation, bent prongs or taped repairs.
- Fully uncoil long cords. A coiled cord under load can overheat.
- Use cord covers or ramps where guests walk. Our [cords on grass guide](/blog/cords-on-grass-outdoor-lighting-safety) covers lawn-specific tactics.
- Keep cords away from sprinkler heads. Turn irrigation off for the day.

## Heaters and cooling equipment

Heaters, fans and air conditioners draw more power than lights and are the most common cause of overloaded cords. Plug large equipment directly into a wall outlet or a heavy-duty cord with no splitters. See [fans and coolers](/blog/fans-and-coolers-for-summer-events).

## Chicago notes

Weather changes quickly here. A dry afternoon can become a damp evening by the lake. Check the forecast and bring covers. Rental tents with sidewalls change the airflow and can trap heat around cords, so keep cords in open areas. If your event is on public property, ask the park district about electrical rules; some parks allow only their own outlets.

## Frequently asked questions

### What gauge extension cord do I need outdoors?

Use 12 gauge for long runs or heavy loads, 14 gauge for moderate ones, and 16 gauge only for short light runs. Check the amp rating on the label.

### Can I use an indoor cord outside?

No. Indoor cords lack weather-resistant insulation and can crack, short or shock. Use cords marked for outdoor use.

### Do I need a GFCI outside?

Yes. Outdoor outlets should be GFCI protected, either built in or with a portable adapter.

### How do I keep cord connections dry?

Raise them off the ground, cover them with a weatherproof connection box, and leave a drip loop in the cord.

### Can I daisy-chain extension cords?

Avoid it. Each connection adds resistance and risk. Use one cord of the right length.

### Can cords stay out in the rain?

Outdoor-rated cords handle rain, but connections should be covered and off standing water. If the rain is heavy, shut off power at the source.

### How do I stop guests from tripping on cords?

Route them along edges and fences, and use cord covers where they must cross paths.
