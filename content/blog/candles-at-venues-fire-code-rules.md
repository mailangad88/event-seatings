**Quick answer**

Many venues allow candles only when the flame is enclosed in glass, and some ban open flame entirely. Rules come from the fire code your local fire marshal enforces plus the venue's own policy and insurance, so they differ from building to building. Ask your venue in writing before you order candles, and have an LED option ready. Never rely on a florist or a rental company to know the rule.

</div>

Few details change the feel of a table as much as candlelight, and few details cause more last-minute scrambles. A couple buys 200 votives, the venue manager says no open flame the week before, and the table plan changes. This guide explains how candle rules work in general, what to ask, and how to get a flame-like glow without the risk.

## Why rules differ

Candle rules come from three layers:

1. **The local fire code.** Illinois communities adopt fire codes through their fire districts or municipalities, and each can set its own rules for open flame in assembly spaces, tents and public buildings.
2. **The venue's policy.** A hotel, museum or historic building often bans or limits open flame beyond what the code requires.
3. **The venue's insurance.** Even where code allows candles, the insurer may not.

Because the layers stack, the strictest one wins. A suburban banquet hall may allow votives in glass, while a downtown loft allows none. Do not assume what one venue permits applies to another. Always check with your venue and your local fire department or fire prevention bureau.

## What to ask the venue

<div class="table-wrap">

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Is open flame allowed indoors, outdoors, or both? | Policies often split by area |
| Must candles be enclosed in glass? | Votives in open dishes are often refused |
| How tall must the glass be? | Taller glass protects from drafts |
| How far from linens, greenery or draping? | Dry greenery ignites easily |
| Are taper candles allowed? | Many venues ban them |
| Who lights and extinguishes them? | Some require staff only |
| Is there a fee or permit? | Some jurisdictions require approval |

## Practical safety habits

When candles are allowed, these habits lower risk. They are general good practice, not a substitute for the venue's rules.

- Use stable holders that sit flat. Wide bases do not tip.
- Keep flames away from paper, florals with dry stems, and hanging fabric.
- Place candles where guests will not lean over them. Low centerpieces beat tall ones with open flames.
- Never leave burning candles unattended. Assign someone to check them before the doors open and after dinner.
- Plan an extinguisher or a fire blanket location, and know where the venue's extinguishers are.

## Candle alternatives that look good

Flameless options have improved. They run on batteries, can be dimmed by remote and hold up in wind.

- **LED votives and pillars.** Warm bulbs with a flicker setting are the closest to real flame. Choose 2200K to 2700K for a natural look.
- **Battery lanterns.** See [lanterns and luminaries](/blog/lanterns-and-luminaries-for-events) for sizes and layouts.
- **Fairy lights in a jar.** Cheap and soft, with no flame.
- **Pin spot over the table.** A [pin spot](/blog/pin-spot-lighting-centerpieces) puts light on the centerpiece without anything burning.
- **Glass hurricanes with LED inserts.** The glass preserves the traditional look.

## Outdoor and tent candles

Outdoors, wind blows flames sideways and snuffs them, and tents add fire concerns because fabric is flammable. Many tent companies and fire officials restrict open flame inside tents. If you plan to use candles in a rented tent, ask the tent company and the fire marshal first. For tents, flameless candles are the safer default. For more on [tent lighting](/blog/topic/tents), see our tent guides.

## Chicago specifics

The City of Chicago and each suburb enforce their own fire prevention rules, and a venue in the city can differ from one in a nearby village. We have not listed any single ordinance here because rules change. Ask the venue first, then the fire prevention bureau of the municipality or fire protection district if the venue is not sure.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use candles at my wedding venue?

Maybe. Many venues allow candles in enclosed glass and ban open flame otherwise. Ask your venue coordinator for the exact policy and get it in writing.

### Are LED candles allowed everywhere?

Almost always, because they have no flame. They are the safe default for tents, historic buildings and rooms with heavy draping.

### Do I need a permit for candles?

Some jurisdictions require a permit or approval for open flame in assembly spaces. Check with the venue and your local fire prevention bureau.

### Can I use taper candles?

Taper candles have open flames and tip easily, so many venues ban them. Ask first, or use LED tapers.

### How many candles per table?

There is no universal number. Many venues set their own limit, so ask for it and design the table around that limit.

### What are the best candle alternatives?

Warm flickering LED votives, battery lanterns, and a pin spot over the centerpiece all give a candle-like glow.

### Do candles work outdoors in Chicago?

Wind off the lake and open prairie blows them out, so use tall glass or switch to LEDs for reliable lighting.
