Activity directors at senior living communities and nursing homes plan more events than almost anyone else we work with: family visit days, holiday parties, resident birthdays and anniversaries, and regular community room gatherings throughout the year. The guest list is often smaller than a wedding or corporate party, but the room has to work for residents who use walkers, wheelchairs or canes, and for staff who need to set up and break down quickly between other activities.

## What matters most: space, not size

A community room event is rarely about fitting in the most people. It's about making sure every resident can get to their seat, sit comfortably, and get back out, without anyone squeezing past a table corner or stepping around a cord. Before you pick furniture, walk the room and note the doorway width, any tight turns, and where the walking path needs to stay clear.

## Chair comfort and mobility clearance

Pick chairs with a seat height that's easy to get into and out of, armrests where possible, and a stable base that won't tip or slide. Space chairs so there's real room between rows, not just enough to squeeze a cane through. A good rule is to leave at least one full wheelchair-width aisle through the middle of any seating area, and keep a clear path from the seating to the door. Our [ADA accessible seating and pathways guide](/blog/ada-accessible-event-seating-and-pathways) goes into aisle widths and clearances in more detail, and it's worth reading before you finalize a layout.

## Lightweight furniture that staff can move alone

Activity staff often set up and break down a room by themselves between shifts. Heavy banquet chairs or oversized tables slow that down and can be a safety risk for staff moving them solo. We lean toward folding chairs and tables that one person can carry and stack without help, which matters more here than in almost any other setting we serve. The [garden folding chair](/chairs/garden-folding) is a common fit for this kind of room because it's light and stacks easily between events.

## Family visit days and holiday parties

Family visit days tend to bring in more chairs than a typical weekday, since residents' families join for a few hours at once. Holiday parties are usually the biggest events of the year for a community, often filling the dining room or main activity space with round tables for a shared meal. Our [how many chairs do I need guide](/blog/how-many-chairs-do-i-need) can help an activity director figure out seating counts once you know how many families are expected, and the [tablecloth size chart](/blog/tablecloth-size-chart) matches linens to whatever round or rectangular tables the room already has or that we bring in.

## Birthdays, anniversaries and small celebrations

Not every event needs a full room setup. A resident's 90th birthday or a wedding anniversary might just need a few extra chairs, a small table for a cake or cards, and enough room for family to gather around one resident comfortably. Keep these setups simple and leave the rest of the room's usual layout alone so residents who aren't part of the celebration aren't disrupted.

## Booking around the community's own calendar

Senior living communities run on a tight activities calendar, often with the dining room double-booked for meals before and after an event. Tell us your setup and breakdown windows and we'll plan delivery around them. If your building restricts outside vendor access to certain doors or hours, pass that along too so nothing holds up setup on the day.

## Frequently asked questions

### What kind of chairs work best for a senior living event?

Lightweight chairs with a comfortable, slightly higher seat height and armrests where possible. They should be easy for one staff member to carry and stack, and stable enough that they won't shift when someone leans on the arm to sit or stand.

### How wide should aisles be for walkers and wheelchairs?

Wide enough for a wheelchair to pass comfortably without bumping a table or chair, with no narrow pinch points. Ask us for a layout that keeps those paths clear before the order is finalized.

### Can activity directors book rentals directly?

Yes. We work with activity directors and events staff directly, and we can set up a repeat order for events that happen every year, like a holiday party.

### Do you provide round or rectangular tables for a community room?

Both, depending on the room and the event. Round tables suit a shared meal, while rectangular tables work well for a buffet line or a craft activity.

### How far in advance should a senior living community book?

As soon as the date is set, especially around the holidays when many communities and venues are booking at once. A few weeks of notice gives us room to confirm the right chair and table counts.

### What should we avoid in the room layout?

Avoid narrow walking paths, loose cords across the floor, and low table legs or decor placed where someone using a walker might not see them. Keep the main path from the door to the seating area completely clear.
