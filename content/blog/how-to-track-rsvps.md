**Quick answer**

Pick one place for replies, such as a website, a form or a single email address. Set a reply date two to three weeks before the event, and a few days before your caterer's deadline. Log every answer in one sheet with status, party size and notes. Send one friendly reminder, then call the stragglers personally so the final count is firm.

## Choose one intake point

The biggest RSVP problem is scatter. Ask guests to reply in one way and say so on the invitation: an online form, a dedicated email address, a phone number or a reply card. If someone texts you anyway, accept it and log it, but gently point them to the main method next time. Our comparison of [digital versus paper invitations](/blog/digital-vs-paper-invitations) helps you pick the channel.

## Set the reply date backward from your vendors

Ask your caterer when they need a final count. Then set the reply date a few days earlier, so you have time to chase. For events with rentals, see [the final headcount deadline](/blog/final-headcount-deadline-event-rentals), because chairs, tables and plates are often counted at the same time.

## Build a tracker with the right columns

A useful sheet has these columns: household name, number invited, number attending, status, meal or dietary notes, children, plus-one name, contact, and notes. Use four statuses: yes, no, maybe and no reply. Add a total row for each so the count updates by itself.

## Ask for what you need on the reply

If you need meal choices, allergies, accessibility needs or ages of children, ask for them in the form. It is much easier to collect them once than to chase them later. For inclusive planning ideas, see our [inclusive event planning checklist](/blog/inclusive-event-planning-checklist).

## Follow up in layers

1. A group reminder about halfway to the deadline.
2. A second note a few days before the deadline, to those who have not replied.
3. A personal text or call to the rest after the deadline.

Keep the tone light: "We are finalizing our count, could you let us know by Friday?" Do not scold. Many people forget more than they decline.

## Handle the maybes and the late replies

Decide your policy in advance. A common approach is to treat a maybe as a no after the deadline unless you ask them to confirm. For late yes replies, accept them if your vendor and room can handle it, and note the added costs. Keep a few spare seats in your plan for this.

## Keep a day-of copy

Print the final attendee list for your door helper, and include the meal choices if you have a plated dinner. Share the count with your caterer, bartender and rental company. If anything changes in the final days, send everyone the updated number in writing.

## Chicago-area notes

Many Chicago-area guests need extra lead time for travel, parking and winter weather, so a clear date and location help them reply. If your event is on a busy weekend, such as a marathon or festival weekend, mention parking and transit when you invite. Some venues and park districts limit guest numbers, so check the room limit before you send invitations and keep your yes count under it. For outdoor events with a tent, include a rain plan on the invitation so replies are not held up by weather worries.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I keep track of RSVPs?

Use one intake method and one sheet. Log each reply as it arrives, with a status, a count and any notes. Review the sheet weekly and send a reminder before your deadline.

### When should the RSVP deadline be?

Many hosts set it two to three weeks before the event and a few days before the caterer's deadline. Larger events and weddings often need more time. Ask your vendors first.

### What do I do about people who do not RSVP?

Send one friendly group reminder, then contact them directly by text or phone. Treat no reply as a no after your final call. Tell your caterer the number you are confident in.

### Is it rude to follow up on an RSVP?

No. Most people are busy and forget. A short, warm note asking for a reply by a date is normal, and guests usually appreciate it.

### How do I track RSVPs for free?

A shared spreadsheet or a free online form works well. Add columns for status, party size and dietary needs. A printable tracker is also fine for smaller events.

### What should an RSVP include?

The reply date, how to respond, the number attending and any choices you need, such as meals or allergies. For events with kids, ask for ages if it affects food or seating.

### How do I count plus-ones and kids?

Record adults and children separately, since they affect food, chairs and seating. List the plus-one name when you can. If an invitation was addressed to specific names, track only those names.
