A guest list feels simple until you start writing it. Then a cousin's new partner, a coworker you like and a neighbor who invited you last year all appear at once, and the list grows past the room. The fix is not a longer list or a shorter one. It is a process: set the cap first, build tiers, make rules, and apply them evenly so every decision is easy to explain.

## Start with the cap, not the names

Your cap comes from three things: budget, space and purpose. Food cost scales with each guest, and so do chairs, tables and plates. A room that fits 80 people at tables might fit only 50 comfortably with a dance floor and a buffet. Our [floor plan for 50 guests](/blog/floor-plan-for-50-guests) and [floor plan for 100 guests](/blog/floor-plan-for-100-guests) help you see what fits.

## Build the master list in tiers

Write every name you can think of, then sort them.

- **Tier A:** immediate family, the guest of honor's closest friends, anyone whose absence would be a hole in the event.
- **Tier B:** extended family, close friends, colleagues you work with often.
- **Tier C:** people you would enjoy seeing but whom the event does not need.

Invite Tier A first. As replies and declines come in, send invitations to Tier B, then C. For this to work, invitations must go out early enough that the last wave still has a few weeks. See [how to track RSVPs](/blog/how-to-track-rsvps).

## Decide your plus-one rule

A common approach is to give plus-ones to married, engaged and long-term partners, and to anyone traveling a long distance, and not to others. Whatever you choose, apply it to everyone. Tell people how the invitation is addressed so no one is surprised.

## Decide your kids rule

If children are welcome, count them in chairs, plates and a kids table. If they are not, say so kindly on the invitation or by word of mouth. Mixed situations are easier when you offer a reason, such as venue rules or a late hour. Our guide to [kids tables and chairs for events](/blog/kids-tables-and-chairs-for-events) helps if kids are coming.

## Handle the awkward cases

Coworkers: invite everyone on a team or no one. Exes and family tension: think about seating and about how the guest of honor feels, and do not decide on their behalf. Co-hosts and parents who want to add names: give each a number of slots and let them choose within it.

## Cut fairly

If you must trim, cut by category, not by person. For example, no coworkers, or no second cousins. A category rule is easier to explain and feels less personal.

## Plan for declines

Not everyone who is invited attends. Many hosts expect a share to decline, which is why waves work well. Do not over-invite on the assumption that people will decline, though, since a full yes is possible. Keep the cap as a hard stop for what you can afford and seat.

## Build your guest list in an hour

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Set the cap.</strong> Use the budget and the room, not the length of your wish list.</li>
<li><strong>Brain dump.</strong> Write every name, with no editing yet.</li>
<li><strong>Sort into tiers.</strong> A, B and C, with a note on household and relationship.</li>
<li><strong>Apply your rules.</strong> Plus-ones, kids and coworkers, written down.</li>
<li><strong>Send in waves.</strong> Tier A first, then fill the gaps as replies arrive.</li>

## Chicago-area notes

Chicago-area families are often spread across the city and suburbs, and many events include out-of-town relatives, so plan for travel. Send save-the-dates early if you expect guests flying into O'Hare or Midway. Parking, transit and winter weather affect who can come, so mention them in your invitation. A venue in a village may have its own occupancy limit, and some park district rooms are small. Ask what the capacity is and whether it counts staff and vendors. For a house or backyard party, consider neighbors and street parking before you set the cap.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I make a guest list for a party?

Set a cap based on budget and space, then write every possible guest and sort them into tiers. Invite the top tier first and the rest as replies arrive. Decide plus-one and kid rules before you start.

### How many people should I invite to my event?

Start from what your budget and venue can handle, then invite a little more than your target if you expect declines. Many hosts see some guests decline, but you cannot count on it. Keep a hard cap for the number you can seat and feed.

### How do I cut down a guest list?

Cut by category instead of person, such as extended family or coworkers. Apply the rule to everyone. Share the reason kindly, such as venue size or budget.

### Should I give everyone a plus-one?

You do not have to. A common approach is to include partners who are married, engaged or in a long relationship, and anyone traveling alone. Apply the same rule to every guest.

### How do I say no kids politely?

Say it clearly on the invitation or in a quick personal message, and give a simple reason such as the venue or the late hour. Offer a suggestion like a sitter list for out-of-town families if you can.

### Do I have to invite all coworkers?

Invite either everyone in a team or no one from it. Picking favorites creates awkwardness. If budget is the limit, say the event is for family and close friends only.

### How early should I send invitations?

Four to six weeks is common for parties and eight to twelve weeks for larger or travel events. Ask for a reply date before your caterer's deadline.
