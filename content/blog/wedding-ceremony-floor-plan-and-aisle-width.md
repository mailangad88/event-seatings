**Quick answer**

Make the center aisle 5 to 6 feet wide (4 feet is the minimum for a processional with a bridal party), set chair rows 32 to 36 inches apart front to back, and use 18 to 20 inches of width per chair. A 144-chair ceremony in two blocks of 8 across and 9 rows needs about 34 x 38 feet, including the altar space.

</div>

A ceremony floor plan is simple on paper and surprisingly easy to get wrong in person. The aisle is too narrow for a dress and two ushers, the rows are too close for older guests to get in and out, or the chairs run so far back that guests in row 20 cannot see. Whether you are in a church, a Chicago lakefront park or a backyard in the suburbs, the same measurements work.

## Aisle width: what you actually need

An aisle that looks wide at a site visit shrinks once chairs go out and guests turn to look. Here is a quick guide.

| Aisle width | Works for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 3 ft | One person | Too tight for a bridal party or a wheelchair |
| 4 ft | Two people side by side | Minimum for a processional with ushers |
| 5 to 6 ft | Bride plus escort, a gown with a train | Ideal for most ceremonies |
| 8 ft or more | Large processional, cultural ceremony, baraat | Common for big halls and tents |

Keep in mind that your aisle, your side aisles and any wheelchair route must also work for guests entering and exiting. The 36-inch minimum clear path for wheelchairs applies at any narrow point. See [ADA accessible event seating and pathways](/blog/ada-accessible-event-seating-and-pathways).

## Row spacing and seat width

Rows need enough space for guests to pass without everyone standing. A common standard is 32 to 36 inches from the back of one chair to the back of the next, which is about 2.7 to 3 feet. Chairs are typically 16 to 18 inches wide, so allow 18 to 20 inches per seat. In a side block of 8 chairs, that is about 12 to 13 feet wide.

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Set the altar.</strong> Leave 8 to 10 feet of floor in front for the officiant, couple and wedding party.</li>
<li><strong>Mark the center aisle.</strong> String or tape, 5 to 6 feet wide.</li>
<li><strong>Set row one.</strong> Place it 8 to 10 feet from the altar.</li>
<li><strong>Space the rows.</strong> 32 to 36 inches back to back.</li>
<li><strong>Add side aisles.</strong> 3 feet along the walls or tents, 4 feet if guests will cross to seats.</li>
</ol>

## Family rows and reserved seating

The first two rows are usually reserved for parents and grandparents. Use ribbon, small signs or a flower on the chair end. If parents are divorced, see [seating for divorced parents and blended families](/blog/seating-chart-divorced-parents-blended-families) before you decide which side people sit on. For a larger ceremony, [reserved seating and signage](/blog/reserved-seating-and-signage-layout) explains how to mark rows.

## Outdoor ceremonies in Chicago

Outdoor ceremonies near Chicago have a few extra rules: soft ground, wind, sun and sunset times. Chairs on grass settle, so use [chairs on grass](/blog/chairs-on-grass-sand-gravel) advice, aisle runners or boards. Face the couple so the sun is not in the guests' eyes. For parks, ask the park district or municipality about permits for ceremonies with chairs and arches; rules vary by park, so check with the one you are using. A rain plan is a must, as detailed in [outdoor event chairs and Chicago weather](/blog/topic/seasons-and-venues).

## Flipping the room afterward

If the ceremony and reception are in the same room, the crew will move the chairs to the rounds during cocktail hour. Plan the aisle so the chairs can be carried out toward the tables without crossing cocktail guests. See [ceremony to reception room flip layout](/blog/ceremony-to-reception-room-flip-layout). For the chair choice, our guide to [ceremony vs reception chairs](/blog/ceremony-vs-reception-chairs) shows what to use for each.

## Chairs for the ceremony

A [garden folding chair](/chairs/garden-folding) is the most economical option for rows of 100 or more and looks tidy in an aisle. A [cross-back](/chairs/cross-back) looks more finished in photos for a smaller ceremony. Whichever you choose, count carefully: see [how many chairs you need](/blog/how-many-chairs-do-i-need).

## Frequently asked questions

### How wide should a wedding ceremony aisle be?

Five to six feet is ideal. Four feet is the minimum for a processional with two people side by side.

### How far apart should ceremony rows be?

32 to 36 inches from the back of one chair to the back of the next. Closer than 30 inches and guests cannot pass.

### How many chairs fit in a row?

Eight to ten chairs per block of one aisle is typical. More than ten makes it hard to reach the middle seats.

### How much space do I need for 100 ceremony guests?

About 600 to 800 square feet in rows, plus space for the altar and aisle. A 25 x 35 foot space works.

### Where do the parents sit at a ceremony?

In the first row on each side, nearest the aisle. Reserve the row with a sign or ribbon.

### Do I need an aisle runner?

No, but it helps on grass and defines the aisle. See our aisle runner guidance for lengths and materials.
