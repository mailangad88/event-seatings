**Quick answer**

A common rule for a cookie swap is that each guest brings 6 cookies for every guest (so 12 guests means 72 cookies of one kind) and leaves with 6 of each type. Give each cookie its own platter and a label that names the cookie and lists common allergens. Set up one 6-foot table for every 8 to 10 cookie types, with boxes at one end, and keep coffee and cocoa on a separate table.

</div>

A cookie exchange is an easy party. Everyone bakes one kind, brings a lot of it and goes home with a mixed box. It works for 8 or for 30, in a living room or a church hall. In Chicago, the weekend before Christmas fills up fast, and early December Saturdays are easy to book. The hard part is not baking. It is the table, the boxes and the labels. This guide explains how much to ask guests to bring, how to lay out the display, and what to rent.

## How many cookies per guest

The classic rule is simple. Each guest brings enough of one kind of cookie to give each other guest the same number. Six per guest is a common choice, and some hosts use a dozen for a bigger box.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Guests | Cookie types | Each guest brings | Each guest takes home |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 8 | 48 (4 dozen) | 6 of each, 48 total |
| 12 | 12 | 72 (6 dozen) | 6 of each, 72 total |
| 20 | 20 | 120 (10 dozen) | 6 of each, 120 total |
| 30 | 30 | 180 (15 dozen) | 6 of each, 180 total |

</div>

That is a lot of baking at 20 or 30 guests. For bigger groups, cut it to 4 or 3 per type, or split guests into pairs who bake together. Ask guests to bring a few extra for tasting at the party, plus a plate of their own to display. Rules of thumb only, so adjust for your group.

If you want a looser swap, ask each guest to bring 2 dozen and take home a mixed dozen.

- **One 6-foot table** holds about 8 to 10 platters. An 8-foot table holds more.
- **Put boxes first.** Guests pick up an empty box at one end and fill it as they go.
- **Tongs or small tongs.** One pair per platter, or guests use wax paper.
- **Heights.** Raise a few platters with cake stands or boxes under the cloth. See [cake stands](/blog/cake-stand-sizes-for-wedding-cakes) and [centerpiece risers](/blog/centerpiece-risers-and-stands-guide).
- **Cloth.** A cloth to the floor hides boxes of extra cookies under the table. See the [tablecloth size chart](/blog/tablecloth-size-chart).
- **Separate drinks.** Coffee and cocoa go on another table so they stay away from the cookies. See [hot cocoa bar](/blog/hot-cocoa-bar-winter-event) and [coffee urns](/blog/how-many-coffee-urns-for-a-party).

## Boxes and bags

Choose boxes before the party, and have a few more than your guest count.

- **Bakery boxes or tins** protect the cookies. Plan one per guest plus a few spares.
- **Wax paper or parchment** between layers keeps frosting from sticking.
- **Separate soft and crisp.** Soft cookies make crisp ones soft.
- **Take-home bags** for the cookies that do not fit.
- **A spot for guests to write their name.** Label tape or a marker.

## Labels and allergens

This is the part to take seriously. Cookies often contain nuts, eggs, milk, wheat and soy, and some guests cannot eat them at all. A good label is simple.

1. Name of the cookie.
2. The baker's name.
3. A short list of common allergens: peanuts, tree nuts, milk, eggs, wheat, soy.
4. Words like "gluten-free" only if the baker knows the ingredients and the kitchen is clean. Otherwise do not write it.

Give guests small cards in advance, and ask them to fill them out and tape them to the platter. For more, see [dietary restrictions planning](/blog/dietary-restrictions-planning-for-events) and [signage and easels](/blog/signage-and-easels-for-events). Keep nut cookies on their own platter, and use separate tongs. Tell guests with severe allergies that the cookies were baked in home kitchens.

## Seating and rentals

A cookie exchange is a short party, about 2 hours. Guests walk around, taste and visit. Plan seats for about half the guests, and a few cocktail tables for plates and cups. For 12 guests, a living room with a table is fine. For 20 or more, rent tables and chairs.

## What most hosts find out too late

- **Cookies need room to breathe.** Platters stacked on top of each other crush them. Give each platter its own patch of table.
- **Warm rooms melt frosting.** Keep the room cool, away from the oven and the heater.
- **Chicago in December means coats, boots and snow.** Plan a coat spot near the door. See [coat racks](/blog/coat-racks-for-winter-events-chicago).
- **Peak December Saturdays book early.** Ask for tables and chairs soon after you set your date.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many cookies does each person bring to a cookie exchange?

A common rule is 6 cookies for each guest. For 12 guests that is 72 cookies, or 6 dozen, of one kind. For a larger party, cut it to 3 or 4 per guest.

### How many guests should I invite to a cookie swap?

Between 8 and 20 is easy. More than 20 means a lot of baking, so ask guests to bring fewer per person. Plan about 2 hours.

### How do I label cookies for allergies?

Write the name, the baker and the common allergens: peanuts, tree nuts, milk, eggs, wheat and soy. Do not call a cookie gluten-free unless you are sure. Keep nut cookies on their own platter.

### What do I need for a cookie exchange table?

Platters, labels, tongs, boxes, a long cloth and a separate drink table. One 6-foot table holds 8 to 10 platters. Boxes go at the start.

### How do I keep cookies fresh at the party?

Cover extras and keep the room cool. Separate soft and crisp cookies in boxes. Replenish platters from a box under the table.

### Do I need to rent chairs for a cookie exchange?

For 12 or fewer, home chairs often work. For 20 or more, plan chairs for about half the guests and a couple of cocktail tables.
