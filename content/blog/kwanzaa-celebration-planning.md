Kwanzaa is a community-centered African American cultural holiday, created in 1966 by Maulana Karenga, that celebrates heritage, family and values. It falls in the busiest, most tired week of the year. The planning pain is mostly this: how to hold a meaningful gathering, not just a party, when schedules are crowded and people are traveling. This guide offers a simple structure for a home or community karamu that is easy to adapt.

## The principles and the symbols

Each day of Kwanzaa honors one of the Nguzo Saba (seven principles): Umoja (unity), Kujichagulia (self-determination), Ujima (collective work and responsibility), Ujamaa (cooperative economics), Nia (purpose), Kuumba (creativity) and Imani (faith). Each evening a candle is lit on the kinara, the candle holder, starting with the black candle in the center, then alternating red on the left and green on the right.

The Kwanzaa table typically holds seven symbols: the mkeka (a woven mat), the kinara, the mishumaa saba (seven candles), mazao (fruits and vegetables, representing the harvest), muhindi (ears of corn, one for each child in the household), kikombe cha umoja (the unity cup) and zawadi (gifts, often handmade or books). Colors are red, black and green. Families and communities decorate in different ways, often adding African cloth, art, books and family photos.

Kwanzaa is cultural, not religious, and many families who observe it also celebrate Christmas, Hanukkah or other holidays. Some people attend a community event, some host at home, and some do both. Ask your community or local cultural organization how they hold it.

## Planning the karamu

The karamu, or feast, is traditionally held on the evening of December 31. Many communities hold it as a big potluck with music, drumming, dance, poetry and storytelling. A typical order is a welcome, a libation (pouring water or juice in remembrance of ancestors, a practice you can explain to guests), a call and response, the lighting of the candles and the day's principle, a cultural program, and the meal. After the meal, there may be a gift exchange and dancing. Keep the program to 45 to 60 minutes before the food comes out, so children and elders stay engaged.

**The menu.** Many families serve dishes from across the African diaspora: jollof rice, collard greens, black-eyed peas, sweet potatoes, cornbread, fried or baked chicken, fish, peas and rice, plantains, gumbo, and sweet potato pie. A potluck lets everyone contribute their family dishes. Label allergens and keep vegetarian options. Use [buffet table layouts](/blog/buffet-table-layouts) to plan the flow.

**Seating.** A circle or a U shape of chairs suits ceremony and storytelling, and long tables suit the meal. If the room allows, set the Kwanzaa table at the front or center on a mat so everyone sees it. Provide seats for elders near the front. For larger gatherings, use round tables of eight to ten. See [how many chairs do I need](/blog/how-many-chairs-do-i-need).

**Activities.** A children's activity corner with books, crafts and a principle of the day card. A table for family photos or an ancestor wall. An African marketplace (soko) with local artisans and vendors is a nice way to practice Ujamaa, cooperative economics.

## Respectful practice

Learn the meaning of each part before you present it, especially if you are hosting an event for a school or workplace. Invite community elders or a local cultural organization to lead the libation and explain the history. Avoid treating Kwanzaa as a decoration theme only. Greet guests with "Habari gani?" (What is the news?) and the response of the day's principle, such as "Umoja!" Keep the tone warm, educational and welcoming to guests who are new to it.

## Chicago-area notes

Chicago has a rich African American community and many cultural centers, churches, libraries, schools and neighborhood groups that host Kwanzaa events between Christmas and New Year's. Winter weather is the main factor. Plan for snow, ice and coats, and see [coat racks for winter events](/blog/coat-racks-for-winter-events-chicago). Because many people travel at the end of December, keep the guest count flexible and the RSVP simple. If you plan an outdoor event, a heated tent can work, but check permits with your village or park district. Venues often discount the week between Christmas and New Year's, so ask.

## Frequently asked questions

### When is Kwanzaa?

Kwanzaa is celebrated from December 26 to January 1 each year. The karamu feast is on December 31, and January 1 is a day of reflection.

### What are the seven symbols of Kwanzaa?

They are the mkeka (mat), kinara (candle holder), mishumaa saba (seven candles), mazao (crops), muhindi (corn), kikombe cha umoja (unity cup) and zawadi (gifts).

### What are the seven principles of Kwanzaa?

Umoja (unity), Kujichagulia (self-determination), Ujima (collective work and responsibility), Ujamaa (cooperative economics), Nia (purpose), Kuumba (creativity) and Imani (faith).

### What is a karamu?

Karamu is the Kwanzaa feast, traditionally held on December 31. It often includes a program of music, dance, poetry and libation, followed by a communal meal.

### How do I light the kinara?

Start with the black center candle on the first night, then light a candle from the left (red) on the second night, then from the right (green), alternating each night.

### Can anyone celebrate Kwanzaa?

Kwanzaa was created to celebrate African American and African heritage, and people of all backgrounds are often welcome to attend community celebrations. If you are not part of the community, take part respectfully and learn the meaning.

### What food is served at Kwanzaa?

Dishes from across the African diaspora, such as jollof rice, greens, black-eyed peas, cornbread, sweet potatoes and fish. A potluck is common.

### How do I plan a Kwanzaa party for kids?

Keep the program short and interactive. Let kids light a candle, read the principle, make crafts and help set the table.
