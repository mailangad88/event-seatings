**Quick answer**

Build an iftar around sunset (maghrib). Have dates, water and a light starter on the tables before the call to prayer, allow a prayer break, then serve the main meal in a smooth line. Plan about one seat per guest, an extra 10 percent of food, a separate prayer space and a volunteer for every 15 to 20 guests. Check the exact sunset time for your date.

A community iftar looks simple, and then 200 hungry people arrive at the same minute. The fast ends at sunset, so there is no flexible start time, and the serving window is tight. The usual problems are food that runs late, lines that stretch past the prayer, and not enough seats and cups on the tables at the moment the fast breaks. Planning backward from sunset fixes most of it.

## Understand the rhythm of the evening

During Ramadan, many Muslims fast from dawn to sunset and break the fast at maghrib with dates and water, following a tradition associated with the Prophet. Many then pray maghrib together, and have a fuller meal afterward. Some communities serve a light snack first, then pray, then eat; others serve everything in a continuous line. Ask your imam or community leaders what they expect. The plan will vary between mosques, student groups, families and interfaith events.

If you are hosting a mixed or interfaith group, give a short welcome explaining the order. Guests who are not fasting should be welcome and comfortable. If non-Muslim guests join, a quick introduction to the rhythm of breaking the fast is appreciated. Keep alcohol and pork out of the event entirely and ask caterers for halal certification if that is expected by your community.

## Food and quantities

Start with dates, water or milk, and perhaps a soup or fruit, then a main meal. A common approach is to prepare about one plate per guest and add 10 percent for seconds, children and latecomers. For a buffet, plan one line for every 75 to 100 guests, and consider serving by table to avoid a rush right after prayer. Family style platters can speed things up.

Label everything: halal, vegetarian, gluten free, nut free. Many communities serve dishes from several cuisines (South Asian, Arab, Turkish, Somali, Bosnian and more), and an iftar potluck is a lovely way to share them. If you rely on potluck, assign categories so you do not end up with twelve desserts and no rice.

Water matters most. After a long fast, guests drink a lot. Provide pitchers or dispensers on every table, plus extra at the buffet. See [how many beverage dispensers do I need](/blog/how-many-beverage-dispensers-do-i-need) for counts. For hot drinks such as tea or Arabic coffee, use urns; our [coffee urn guide](/blog/how-many-coffee-urns-for-a-party) helps with quantities.

## Seating and the room

Long tables with benches or chairs are common at community iftars, and they allow guests to sit by family. Separate men's and women's seating is the practice in some communities and not in others, so ask your leaders and tell guests in advance. Set aside seating for elders near the entrance, and keep a family table for children. Provide high chairs. For setup layouts, see [Eid celebration seating and rentals](/blog/eid-celebration-seating-and-rentals), which uses similar tables.

## A run of show for the iftar night

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Arrive early.</strong> Set tables with cups, plates, napkins and dates two hours ahead.</li>
<li><strong>Brief volunteers.</strong> Assign who handles water, food, prayer space and cleanup.</li>
<li><strong>Put dates and water out.</strong> Have them on every table at least 10 minutes before sunset.</li>
<li><strong>Break the fast together.</strong> At the call to prayer, a short dua and then dates and water.</li>
<li><strong>Pray, then serve.</strong> Open the buffet after the group prayer, or by table.</li>
<li><strong>Clean up and close.</strong> Collect trash, set aside leftover food for donation, and thank volunteers.</li>
</ol>

## Details people forget

Prayer space: a clean, quiet room or area with enough floor for rows of worshippers, with shoe racks. Wudu (washing) access: check restrooms and sinks, and keep them clear. Parking: large iftars create traffic as soon as the fast breaks. Trash: rent extra bins and have volunteers empty them. Leftovers: partner with a food pantry or arrange take-home containers. Dates: buy more than you think. Children: provide a play area and a snack plan so parents can pray. Accessibility: wheelchair access and seating for those who cannot sit on the floor.

## Chicago-area notes

Ramadan moves about ten days earlier each year, so it can fall in winter, spring or summer, and the sunset time changes a lot: early on a winter evening, very late in summer. Check the exact time for the date. In the Chicago area, many mosques, community centers and university groups host large iftars, and many venues will book out the whole Ramadan season. In winter, plan for coats, snow and parking; in summer, plan for a long, hot day of fasting and a cool room. If you host outdoors, see our [Chicago tent permit guide](/blog/chicago-tent-permit) and check with your village or park district. Noise and parking are the usual neighbor issues.

## Frequently asked questions

### What time is iftar?

Iftar is at sunset (maghrib), so the time changes every day and by location. Check a local prayer timetable for your date. Plan for dates and water to be on the tables a few minutes before.

### How much food do I need for an iftar?

Plan about one full plate per guest and add roughly 10 percent for seconds and latecomers. After a day of fasting, many guests eat a lot. Include plenty of water.

### Should I serve the meal before or after prayer?

It depends on the community. Many break the fast with dates and water, pray maghrib, and then eat the main meal. Others serve a full meal right away. Ask your imam or leadership.

### How many volunteers do I need for an iftar?

A common rule is one volunteer for every 15 to 20 guests, split among setup, serving, drinks, prayer space, children and cleanup.

### What should I serve at an iftar?

Start with dates and water, and add soup, a main dish, rice or bread, and dessert. Halal food is the standard, and many communities share dishes from their home cuisines.

### Can non-Muslims attend an iftar?

Yes. Many iftars welcome neighbors and colleagues, and an interfaith iftar is a common event. Welcome guests and briefly explain the flow of the evening.

### Do I need separate seating for men and women?

Practices differ among communities. Some seat families together and others use separate areas. Ask your leaders and let guests know ahead of time.

### What do I do with leftover iftar food?

Arrange to donate to a food pantry or a shelter, or send containers home with guests. Plan this ahead, because it is hard to organize at the end of a long evening.
