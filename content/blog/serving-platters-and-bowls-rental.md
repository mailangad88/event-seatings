**Quick answer**

Plan one serving platter or bowl per dish for every 8 to 10 guests, and add a second for each popular dish. A 14 to 16-inch platter feeds about 8 to 10 as one dish; a 10 to 12-inch bowl feeds 6 to 8 as a side. For a buffet, one set of serving pieces covers the line. Family-style needs a set on every table. Ask your caterer for the dish list first.

Serving pieces are the part of a dinnerware order that gets underestimated. A caterer arrives with roast chicken and no platter big enough to carry it. Salad piles up in a bowl meant for dips. Whether you are hosting 25 at home in Evanston or 150 at a hall in the western suburbs, a few decisions on count and size keep service smooth. This guide is about planning, not prices; we do not publish dinnerware prices.

## Match the piece to the dish

- **Platters** suit sliced meat, whole fish, roasted vegetables, sandwiches and desserts.
- **Large bowls** suit salads, pasta, grain dishes and fruit.
- **Small bowls** suit sauces, dressings, condiments and sides such as nuts.
- **Gravy boats and pitchers** suit sauces and syrups.
- **Cake stands and tiered trays** suit desserts and charcuterie.

Heavier dishes need rims to catch juices. A flat platter works for dry foods but not for braised or saucy ones.

## Serving utensils

Every platter and bowl needs a spoon, fork or tongs. Count one utensil per dish, plus a spare. Spoons get dropped, and a shared utensil sits in a dirty bowl. Ask whether utensils come with the rental or from the caterer.

## Keeping food hot and cold

China platters cool quickly. Caterers often use chafing dishes for hot food on a buffet and transfer to platters for presentation. For cold dishes outdoors, use bowls set on ice. Our catering equipment pages cover chafers and warmers in detail; start at the [catering equipment topic](/blog/topic/catering-equipment).

## Table space

A 6-foot banquet table can hold about 4 to 6 serving pieces comfortably. A 60-inch round with 8 settings has room for 2 or 3 platters in the center. Tall pieces make it harder to see across the table. Put platters on a runner or a few cake stands to build height without crowding. See [place setting spacing](/blog/place-setting-spacing-measurements).

## Weight and transport

Large platters are heavy when full. If you are carrying food into a venue, check the stairs and elevator. Chicago high-rise venues may need freight elevator time; ask your venue about load-in windows. See [delivery and packing crates](/blog/dinnerware-delivery-and-packing-crates).

## Presentation details that help

Height makes a buffet look abundant. Place a few platters on risers, cake stands or overturned crates covered in linen, and keep the rest flat. Fill dishes about three-quarters full and refill often rather than overloading. Label dishes with small cards, which also helps guests with allergies. Leave a clear 12 to 18 inches in front of each dish so guests have room to serve, and put the spoon handle toward the guest.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many serving platters do I need for 50 guests?

For a buffet with 4 to 5 dishes, plan about 8 to 10 pieces so popular items can be refilled. Family-style needs far more because each table gets a set.

### What size platter feeds 10 people?

A 14 to 16-inch platter holds a main dish for about 8 to 10 people. Heavier or saucy dishes may need a larger platter or two smaller ones.

### Do I need separate serving utensils?

Yes. Each dish needs its own spoon, fork or tongs. Add one spare per dish.

### Can the caterer supply platters?

Many can. Ask what is included so you do not rent duplicates. If the caterer does not provide them, add them to your rental order.

### What do I use for sauces?

Small bowls or gravy boats with ladles. Count one per sauce, and one per table at a family-style dinner.

### How do I keep food from getting cold on platters?

Serve in small batches and refill often. Use warmers or chafing dishes behind the line if your caterer has them.
