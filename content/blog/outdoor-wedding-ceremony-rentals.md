**Quick answer**

An outdoor wedding ceremony needs one chair per guest, a few upholstered chairs for family, an aisle runner or marked path, an arch or focal point, a signing table with two chairs, a sound plan and a backup for rain or heat. Plan about 8 square feet per seated guest including the aisle, so 100 guests need roughly 800 square feet of lawn. Always book a covered or indoor fallback.

</div>

An outdoor ceremony is simple on paper: chairs, an aisle, a spot to stand. In practice, a Chicago-area lawn brings wind off the lake, a sun that moves during the vows and grass that swallows chair legs. The rental list is short, but each item has to earn its place.

## The rental list

**Chairs.** One per guest. On grass, a lightweight chair that doesn't sink is practical, and a garden folding chair costs the least. For a finished look, wood [cross-back chairs](/chairs/cross-back) or clear [ghost chairs](/chairs/ghost) both work outdoors. Chair estimates on this site range from $5 to $7 for garden folding to $8 to $11 for cross-back, and $10 to $14 for ghost. If the chair has to go from the lawn to a tent afterward, read our guide on [ceremony vs reception chairs](/blog/ceremony-vs-reception-chairs).

**Front row.** Reserve two to four upholstered or contrasting chairs for parents and grandparents, tied with a sash or sign.

**Aisle.** A fabric runner, petals or a mowed path. Long runners need stakes or tape so they don't slip. See aisle runner length and material if you have not picked one.

**Focal point.** A wood or metal arch, a chuppah, a floral wall or simply two large planters. Wind matters here: any arch with fabric needs weights or stakes.

**Signing table.** A 6-foot table with a cloth and two chairs, set to one side.

**Sound.** A small PA with a lapel or handheld mic for the officiant. Guests at the back of a 100-person lawn will not hear an unamplified voice, especially with wind or traffic.

**Programs and water.** A table for programs, a basket of fans in summer, a water station, and shade. Large market umbrellas or a tent solve the third.

## Weather: the rental side

Chicago springs and falls swing by 30 degrees in a day. Ask these questions before you pick the date:

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Where do guests go if it rains?</strong> A tent, a hall or the reception room.</li>
<li><strong>Who decides, and when?</strong> Set a go or no-go time at least 24 hours out.</li>
<li><strong>Can the rental order switch?</strong> Ask whether the setup crew can move chairs on short notice.</li>
<li><strong>Is there shade or heat?</strong> Umbrellas, fans, blankets or heaters by season.</li>
</ol>

A permitted tent for the ceremony adds cost but gives you weather certainty. See [what size tent do I need](/blog/what-size-tent-do-i-need) for guidelines, and check your village for tent rules.

## Where outdoor ceremonies happen

Forest preserves, park district gardens, lakefront parks, private lawns and winery or barn venues all host ceremonies in the Chicago area. Each has different rules about chairs, setup times, vehicles on grass and amplified sound. Public parks and forest preserves typically require a permit for private events and may restrict tents, stakes, alcohol and vehicles; contact the managing park district, forest preserve district or city office to confirm. Don't assume a public place allows a setup just because it is pretty.

## What the ceremony costs to set up

We do not publish prices for anything but chairs, so use this as a budget method rather than a quote. Multiply your guest count by the chair estimate, add a line for each of the other items above, and ask each vendor what delivery, setup and pickup add. The biggest swing is usually the weather plan: a tent or indoor backup changes the total more than the chair style does. Ask for the all-in number, including how long the crew stays, so you do not discover a separate fee for moving chairs after the vows.

## A note on guests with limited mobility

Tell the setup crew where wheelchair spaces and aisle seats go before the day. Keep the aisle on firm ground, add a mat or boards if the lawn is soft, and put a few chairs with arms near the end of a row for guests who need help standing. These small choices cost almost nothing and prevent the most common ceremony-day problem.

## Two timing rules

- Plan the ceremony so the sun is not in the guests' eyes or in the couple's faces; walk the site at the same time of day before you decide which way the chairs face.
- Set up chairs the morning of the ceremony, not the day before, to keep dew, sprinklers and wind from messing them up.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many chairs do I need for an outdoor ceremony?

One for each guest, plus roughly 10 percent spares for late arrivals and extra family. If you also have a reception, count that separately.

### What chairs are best for outdoor ceremonies on grass?

Lightweight garden folding chairs are the budget choice. Wood cross-back chairs and clear ghost chairs give a nicer look and work well on firm lawn. Soft or sloped ground may need boards or a different surface.

### How wide should the aisle be?

Aim for 4 to 6 feet so two people can walk side by side or a wheelchair can pass. Wider aisles look grand but use more space.

### Do I need a permit for an outdoor ceremony?

On private property, usually not for the ceremony itself, though tents may need one. On public land, expect a permit through the city, park district or forest preserve. Ask the managing office well before you book.

### What should I do if it rains?

Have a written plan with a covered or indoor alternative, a decision deadline and a rental order that can move. Sort this before the week of the wedding.

### Should I rent a sound system?

Yes for groups over about 40, since voices carry poorly outdoors. Ask your officiant what mic works with their style.
