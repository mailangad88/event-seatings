**Quick answer**

Ask guests what they need, then plan for access (step-free routes, wide aisles, accessible restrooms), seating (varied chairs, space for mobility devices), food (labeled allergens and dietary options), sensory comfort (a quiet spot, lower volume), communication (clear invitations and large print), and respect for faith, language and family needs. Small changes made early cost little and help everyone.

</div>

Every guest list is more varied than the host expects. Someone uses a cane, someone avoids gluten, someone needs a quiet break, someone does not speak English as a first language and someone is bringing a toddler and a grandparent. Inclusive planning is not about perfection. It is about asking early, removing obstacles and giving people choices. This checklist helps you do that without adding stress.

## Ask early and make it easy to answer

Add a line to your invitation or RSVP: "Please tell us anything that would help you enjoy the event." Include a phone number or email for private replies. See [how to track RSVPs](/blog/how-to-track-rsvps). Treat answers with care, keep the information private and act on it.

## Physical access

Check the route from parking or transit to the entrance, the restroom and your seating area. Look for steps, thresholds, gravel, grass, heavy doors and narrow aisles. Aim for step-free access and wide paths, and avoid blocking them with decor. Our guides on [ADA accessible event seating and pathways](/blog/ada-accessible-event-seating-and-pathways) and [ADA restroom considerations](/blog/ada-restroom-considerations-for-events) go deeper. For grass, see [chairs on grass, sand and gravel](/blog/chairs-on-grass-sand-gravel).

## Seating choices

Offer a mix: chairs with arms, higher seats, a few spots with extra space for mobility devices and seats near exits and restrooms. See [comfortable chairs for older guests](/blog/comfortable-chairs-for-older-guests) and [chair weight capacity and seat sizes](/blog/chair-weight-capacity-and-seat-sizes). Do not seat guests with mobility needs in the back corner or behind a pillar. Ask where they would like to sit.

## Food and drink

Ask about allergies and dietary needs, label dishes with common allergens, and give vegetarian, vegan, gluten-free and halal or kosher options when needed. Keep serving utensils separate. Offer nonalcoholic drinks at the same quality as alcoholic ones, such as [mocktails](/blog/mocktail-bar-ideas). Tell your caterer exactly what the allergy situation is, and ask about cross-contact.

## Sensory comfort

Loud music, flashing lights and crowded rooms can be hard for some guests. Offer a quiet space, keep the volume reasonable during meals, give notice before fireworks or loud toasts and avoid strobe lighting. Children and neurodivergent guests may appreciate a calmer corner with simple activities.

## Communication

Use clear, large-print signs and menus. Offer translations if many guests speak another language, and ask a family member to interpret speeches where needed. Use plain language in invitations. Consider printed programs for guests who are hard of hearing and a microphone for speeches.

## Family and cultural needs

Offer a space for nursing or prayer when appropriate, a changing table, high chairs and a kids' area. See [high chairs and baby station](/blog/high-chairs-and-baby-station-at-events). Respect dress, food and time-of-day needs for cultural and faith traditions, and ask the family or community leader if unsure.

## Staff and helpers

Tell your helpers, servers and vendors about the access plan so they can assist. Assign someone as an access contact on the day, with their name and phone listed in the timeline.

This is a general planning aid, not legal advice. Venues may have their own accessibility features and obligations, so ask what they offer and what you must add.

## Chicago-area notes

Chicago's older buildings, historic halls and some church basements and brownstones can have stairs, narrow doors or limited restrooms, so ask early about access. Winter snow and ice can block ramps and paths, so plan for clearing and salting. In summer, outdoor events need shade and water for guests who are sensitive to heat. Public transit options vary by suburb, and accessible rail and bus information can be checked with the transit agencies. Ask your venue or park district about accessible parking and restrooms.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is inclusive event planning?

It means designing an event so guests with different abilities, ages, diets, cultures and languages can take part comfortably. It starts with asking, then removing barriers in access, seating, food, sound and communication.

### How do I make my event accessible?

Choose a venue with step-free access, plan wide paths and accessible restrooms, offer varied seating and ask guests what they need. Tell your vendors and helpers the plan.

### How do I ask guests about dietary needs?

Add a line to your RSVP asking about allergies and dietary needs, with a deadline. Share the information with your caterer and label food on the day.

### What is a sensory-friendly space at an event?

A quiet area with softer lighting and less noise where guests can take a break. It can be a small room or a corner of the venue. Keep it away from speakers.

### How wide should aisles be for wheelchair access?

Many accessibility guides suggest at least 36 inches for a path, with more room for turns. Check the standards for your venue and aim wider for comfort.

### Do I need a translator at my event?

Not always, but if guests speak different languages, a bilingual host, printed translations or a family interpreter can help. Ask guests in the RSVP.

### How can I make seating inclusive?

Offer chairs of different types and heights, leave open spaces for mobility devices, and place seats near exits and restrooms. Ask guests where they want to sit.
