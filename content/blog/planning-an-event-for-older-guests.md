**Quick answer**

To plan for older guests, put comfort first: supportive chairs with arms, tables close to entrances and restrooms, step-free paths, good lighting, sound that is easy to hear over, earlier start times and shorter timelines. Offer simple food, water and places to rest. Ask guests about needs in advance and assign someone to help with seating and transport.

</div>

Older guests are often the people hosts most want in the room, and also the ones whose comfort is easiest to overlook. Small choices, such as a chair with arms, a table near the door or a quieter corner, can decide whether a grandparent stays for dinner or leaves early. Planning for comfort benefits every guest.

## Seating, paths and access

Choose chairs with firm cushions, arms and a seat height that is easy to get out of; see [comfortable chairs for older guests](/blog/comfortable-chairs-for-older-guests), [chair weight capacity and seat sizes](/blog/chair-weight-capacity-and-seat-sizes) and [do you need chair cushions](/blog/do-you-need-chair-cushions). Place tables close to entrances, restrooms and the food, and leave room for walkers and wheelchairs; see [table spacing and aisle clearance](/blog/table-spacing-and-aisle-clearance) and [ADA accessible event seating and pathways](/blog/ada-accessible-event-seating-and-pathways).

Avoid long walks over grass, gravel or sand; see [chairs on grass, sand and gravel](/blog/chairs-on-grass-sand-gravel). If using a tent or temporary flooring, check that surfaces are level and non-slip; see [tent flooring and subfloors](/blog/tent-flooring-and-subfloors).

## Small changes that help

This table matches common challenges with simple fixes. Not every guest needs every item, so ask.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Challenge | Simple fix | Where to plan it |
|---|---|---|
| Standing for long periods | Offer seats everywhere, including cocktail hour | Lounge or low tables |
| Hearing in a noisy room | Keep music lower, seat away from speakers | Floor plan |
| Reading menus and signs | Large print, good light | Signs and menus |
| Mobility aids | Wide aisles, step-free entrances | Table spacing |
| Heat or cold | Shade, fans, heaters, blankets | Weather plan |
| Dietary needs | Soft, low-sodium and diabetic-friendly options | Menu |
| Fatigue | Earlier start, shorter program, quiet space | Timeline |

</div>

Ask guests, or a family member, about specific needs on the RSVP rather than guessing.

## Plan in six steps

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Ask about needs.</strong> Add a line on your RSVP or invitation for access and dietary needs.</li>
<li><strong>Choose the venue with access in mind.</strong> Check doors, restrooms, parking and elevators.</li>
<li><strong>Plan seating.</strong> Reserve seats near the entrance and away from loud speakers; see [seating chart planner](/downloads/seating-chart-planner).</li>
<li><strong>Set a comfortable schedule.</strong> Start earlier and keep toasts short.</li>
<li><strong>Plan food.</strong> Offer easy-to-eat food and plenty of water.</li>
<li><strong>Assign a helper.</strong> One person greets, escorts and checks in.</li>
</ol>

## Timing, food and sound

Many older guests prefer daytime or early evening events. A lunch or afternoon reception can be easier than a late dinner. Choose foods that are simple to eat and offer softer options. Provide water and coffee; see [coffee and tea service rental](/blog/coffee-and-tea-service-rental). Keep speeches short and use a good microphone so everyone can hear; see [sound system for speeches](/blog/sound-system-for-speeches) and [microphone types for events](/blog/microphone-types-for-events). For reunions and gatherings, see [family reunion seating and rentals](/blog/family-reunion-seating-and-rentals).

For celebrations of life, where many older guests attend, see [celebration of life reception rentals](/blog/celebration-of-life-reception-rentals).

## Staying connected during the event

Assign a family member or friend to each older guest who may need help. They can walk the guest to the table, bring a plate and make sure a ride home is arranged. Check in discreetly during the event, and offer a quiet room to rest if one is available. Let guests leave early without fuss. These gestures make older guests feel welcome rather than managed.

## Chicago-area notes

Chicago winters and summers can be hard on older guests. Plan for icy walkways, snow-cleared entrances, coat storage and a nearby drop-off; see [coat racks for winter events](/blog/coat-racks-for-winter-events-chicago). In summer, provide shade, fans and cool water; see [summer event heat plan](/blog/summer-event-heat-plan-chicago) and [fans and coolers for summer events](/blog/fans-and-coolers-for-summer-events). For transit and parking, see [guest parking and transit plan](/blog/guest-parking-and-transit-plan-chicago). Older buildings in the city may have stairs, so confirm step-free routes with the venue.

## Frequently asked questions

### What chairs are best for older guests?

Chairs with arms, firm cushions and stable legs. Avoid low, soft or wobbly seats.

### Where should older guests sit?

Near the entrance, restrooms and food, and away from loudspeakers. Keep paths wide and clear.

### What time of day is best?

Many older guests prefer daytime or early evening. Ask guests what works for them.

### How do I make the venue accessible?

Check for step-free entrances, accessible restrooms, wide paths and nearby parking. Ask the venue to confirm.

### What food should I serve?

Offer simple, easy-to-eat dishes and soft options, with water and non-alcoholic drinks. Ask about dietary needs.

### How loud should the music be?

Keep it low during dinner and speeches so conversation is easy. Save louder music for later.

### How do I ask about guests' needs politely?

Add a simple line to the RSVP asking about seating, mobility or dietary needs, and follow up privately if needed.
