A repast is planned in the worst week of a family's life, usually in a few days, by people who are exhausted. The pain point is not creativity but logistics: how many will come, who will cook, where it will be, and how to feed a crowd when no one has time to think. This guide aims to make it easier, with plain numbers and a short list of choices, and with room for your own traditions.

## What a repast is, and how traditions differ

The word "repast" means a meal, and in many Black American church traditions it describes the gathering after a funeral, often held in the church fellowship hall. Other communities have their own versions: a funeral luncheon, a reception after a burial, a wake with a meal, a mercy meal, a post-funeral gathering at a family home, a Catholic or Orthodox memorial meal, or the Jewish meal of consolation after a burial (see [shiva house setup and meals](/blog/shiva-house-setup-and-meals)). Customs differ by faith, culture and region. Some families keep it very simple and some host a large meal for hundreds. There is no wrong way, only what fits your family and your community.

If you are not sure, ask your pastor, priest, imam, rabbi, funeral director or an older relative what is customary. Many congregations have a hospitality or funeral committee that handles the meal. If you do not belong to one, a funeral director can often recommend caterers or halls.

## How many to expect and how much food

Start with the number of people at the service. A rough planning rule is that 50 to 75 percent will stay for a meal, though this varies with the time of day and the distance people traveled. If the obituary says "all are welcome," expect more. If the service is small or private, expect fewer. Ask the funeral director for an attendance estimate. Overestimating slightly is easier to handle than running short.

For food quantity, a common rule is a full portion per guest for a main meal and slightly less for a light reception. Choose foods that are filling and forgiving: baked chicken, pasta, rice, greens, salads, rolls and a few desserts. Include vegetarian and allergy-friendly options. Offer coffee, water and tea. Use [catering quantity guides](/blog/how-many-chafing-dishes-for-a-buffet) to plan warming equipment, and [serving utensils](/blog/serving-utensils-checklist-for-catering) so each dish has a spoon.

Many families accept food from friends and church members, and a few take donated dishes. Assign someone to organize who brings what, to avoid six desserts and no mains. Label dishes with the family name so containers go home.

## Seating and flow

Seat the immediate family first, ideally near the entrance or the buffet so they can eat and leave when needed. Offer seats for elders and anyone with mobility needs. Plan enough seating for at least half the guests, since many will eat standing or briefly. Put the buffet where a line can form without crowding the room. A table with photos, a guest book or a memory card basket gives guests something to do and comfort.

<li><strong>Call the funeral director.</strong> Ask the service time, expected attendance and recommended halls or caterers.</li>
<li><strong>Choose the place.</strong> Church hall, community room, restaurant or home.</li>
<li><strong>Pick the menu.</strong> Decide between catering, a restaurant, or home-cooked dishes from friends.</li>
<li><strong>Assign a lead.</strong> One person to coordinate the helpers and answer questions.</li>
<li><strong>Order what you need.</strong> Tables, chairs, linens, warming trays, coffee service.</li>
<li><strong>Set up and clean up.</strong> Arrive two hours early, and assign cleanup helpers.</li>
</ol>

## Etiquette and care

Let the family eat first or have a plate set aside. Keep the tone quiet, and avoid loud music. Keep alcohol according to your family and faith customs. Ask a friend to serve as an usher so the family isn't answering questions. Thank the helpers later, as this week is too hard to do it in the moment. If you are a guest, bring a simple dish if asked, and do not stay long unless invited. For smaller family gatherings after a service, a home is often fine; for larger ones, consider a hall.

## Chicago-area notes

In the Chicago area, many repasts happen in church fellowship halls, funeral home reception rooms, banquet halls or restaurants, and many congregations have a hospitality ministry that handles the meal. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is a funeral repast?

A repast is a meal shared by family and friends after a funeral or burial. The tradition is common in many Black American churches, and similar meals are held in many other communities and faiths.

### How many people attend a funeral repast?

Often about 50 to 75 percent of the people at the service stay for the meal. Ask the funeral director for an estimate and plan a bit extra.

### What food is served at a funeral repast?

Filling, comforting dishes such as baked chicken, pasta, rice, vegetables, salads, rolls and desserts, with coffee and water. Include vegetarian options. Many families rely on church members or friends to bring dishes.

### Who pays for the repast?

Usually the family, though some churches provide the meal and many friends contribute dishes. Funeral homes can sometimes help with costs or recommend caterers.

### Where can a repast be held?

Church fellowship halls, funeral home rooms, community centers, restaurants and family homes are common choices. Pick a place that fits the number of guests.

### How long should a repast last?

Usually 1.5 to 2 hours. Guests come, eat, offer condolences and go. Keep the program simple.

### Do I need to rent tables and chairs for a repast?

Not always. Church halls often have tables and chairs. For other spaces, rent enough for at least half the expected guests and a buffet table.

### How soon after the funeral is a repast held?

Usually right after the service or burial, on the same day. Planning begins as soon as the date is set, so ask for help immediately.
