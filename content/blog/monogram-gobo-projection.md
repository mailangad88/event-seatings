A monogram on the dance floor is one of the most photographed details at a wedding, and it is also one of the simplest to get wrong. The image comes out too small, it lands on a dark carpet, or guests stand in the beam and wear the logo on their faces. This guide explains how it works so you can plan it.

## What a gobo is

The word comes from "goes before optics." A gobo is a thin disc, usually metal or glass, with a pattern cut into it. It sits in front of the lamp inside the fixture. A lens focuses the pattern onto a surface. A metal gobo gives a crisp one-color image. A glass gobo can show color or fine detail, and costs more.

Other projection options exist: an LED projector that can show custom images or a short video loop; a rented digital projector; and lasers. A static gobo is the most common choice for a monogram.

## Choosing the size

There is no one size. The vendor calculates it from the fixture's lens and the throw distance. Lenses differ a lot, so a fixture at 12 feet can throw a small circle or a wide one. Ask for a specific diameter and an angle that the vendor has tested.

A rented white or light-toned [dance floor](/blog/topic/dance-floors-and-decor) is the most reliable surface. A high-gloss floor can reflect the fixture itself and wash out the image.

## Where to mount the fixture

- **High and straight down** gives the cleanest, least distorted circle, but needs a truss, ceiling point or tall stand.
- **At an angle from the side** is easier but stretches the image. Pick an angle with the vendor and keep the logo's text readable.
- **Behind the DJ** works for wall projections.
- **Away from the dance floor.** Guests dancing between the light and the floor block the beam. Raising the fixture reduces shadows.

If the venue allows no rigging, ask for a tall freestanding stand. See the rigging notes in [pin spot lighting](/blog/pin-spot-lighting-centerpieces), which has the same rigging questions.

## Designing the artwork

Good monograms are simple. Thin lines and tiny text can blur at a distance.

- Use bold, high-contrast shapes with generous spacing.
- Limit the design to initials, a date or a short name.
- Avoid very thin script that disappears at 10 feet.
- Provide a vector file (an AI, EPS or SVG file) or a high-resolution black-and-white image.
- Ask for a proof before the vendor builds the gobo.

Typical timeline: custom gobos are made to order, so ask for the vendor's lead time at booking. Do not wait until the last week.

## Brightness and room light

A projection needs a dim room. Candles are fine, but bright uplights, a spotlight or sunlight through a tent wall wash it out. Choose the moment: first dance, grand entrance and last song. Turn the pattern off when the dance floor turns into a party. Combine with [LED dance floor lighting](/blog/led-dance-floor-lighting) in a way that does not fight the monogram.

## Power

A gobo light draws modest power. LED fixtures use less than older halogen ones. Plan an outlet near the mount, and see the [event power needs checklist](/blog/event-power-needs-checklist).

## Chicago notes

Chicago's older ballrooms and historic venues sometimes ban stands or taping on the floor. Ask your venue about rigging, floor protection and who may run the fixture.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a gobo?

A gobo is a small stencil placed inside a light so it projects a pattern, like a monogram, onto a surface.

### How big will my monogram be?

It depends on the distance and lens. Ask your vendor for the diameter at your mounting height.

### Can I project on grass?

It is possible but looks poor because the surface is uneven. Use a dance floor or a white mat.

### How far in advance should I order a custom gobo?

Ask the vendor for their lead time when booking. Custom items need time for design, proofs and production.

### Does a monogram work in a tent?

Yes at night with a light floor. Daylight and uplights wash it out.

### Can I change the monogram during the event?

Not with a static gobo. Digital projectors can show changing images.
