**Quick answer**

To make a wedding seating chart, start from your final RSVP list, group guests by relationship, pick table sizes (8 per 60-inch round, 10 per 72-inch), seat the couple and family first, then fill tables with groups who know each other. Mix only where it makes sense, seat 8 to 10 per table, and finalize 2 to 3 weeks before the wedding so your caterer, venue and rental order match.

</div>

Chicago-area couples tend to juggle large extended families, so the seating chart is the one wedding task that mixes math with feelings. A good chart gets everyone a seat near people they like, keeps the family peace and tells the caterer, the venue and the rental company how many tables to set. This is how to do it in an order that avoids rework.

## Table math

Divide the final guest count by the seats per table. Sixty-inch rounds seat 8; 72-inch rounds seat 10. For 112 guests with rounds of 8, you need 14 tables. Add the head or sweetheart table separately. If the count does not divide evenly, aim for tables of 7 to 9 rather than one lonely table of 3. See [how many tables for 50 to 200 guests](/blog/how-many-tables-for-50-100-150-200-guests) and [round table sizes](/blog/round-table-sizes-and-seating-48-60-66-72-inch).

## Handle the tricky parts

**Divorced or tense families.** Seat them at separate tables, both near the couple, with a friendly host at each.

**Singles.** Seat them with groups they know, not at a "singles table."

**Plus-ones and unknown guests.** Seat them with the person who invited them or with a friendly group.

**Guests who need access.** Seat mobility-limited guests near an aisle and the restroom, with extra room for a chair or walker.

**Late changes.** Keep a spare table or two seats open at a few tables so last-minute swaps are easy.

## Assigned seats or just tables?

Table assignments are the most common. Assigned seats help formal events with plated courses, because the caterer needs to know meal choices. They take longer to plan and any change affects the place cards. Tables with open seating suit small, casual weddings.

## Use the room, not just the list

A chart on paper ignores the room. Before you finalize, overlay it on the floor plan. Keep elderly guests away from the speakers and the dance floor, and away from doors that open to cold air in winter. Keep the kids' table near parents but not next to the bar. Put the loudest friend group near the dance floor and the quietest relatives where conversation carries. Check sight lines to the head table or sweetheart table so toasts reach everyone, and leave a clear route from every table to the restrooms and the buffet or bar.

Seat by energy as much as by relationship. A table of cousins who love to dance will help fill the floor, and a table of older guests who like to talk will be happier near a window or a quieter corner.

## A tool and a deadline

You can use paper cut-outs, a spreadsheet or a free online seating planner. Whatever you use, save a version history, because the chart changes up to the last week. Choose one person, often the couple, to hold the master and make all changes, so the caterer, venue and rental company never receive conflicting versions.

## Displaying the chart

Options include an alphabetical list on a mirror or sign, a table-by-table display with cards, or escort cards in a grid. Each needs a table or easel at the entrance. ## Share the chart with your vendors

The caterer needs counts and meal choices per table. The venue needs the table layout. The rental company needs the number and size of tables, and the chair count. Send a clean copy 2 to 3 weeks out, and a final version when the numbers lock. Our [wedding rentals timeline](/blog/wedding-rentals-timeline) shows when to do each.

## Frequently asked questions

### When should I make the seating chart?

After RSVPs close, usually 3 to 4 weeks before the wedding. Draft earlier to spot problems, then finalize 2 to 3 weeks out.

### How many people per table at a wedding?

Eight on a 60-inch round and ten on a 72-inch round is standard. Ten at a 60-inch round is tight.

### Do I need assigned seats?

Assigned tables are enough for most weddings. Assigned seats help if the caterer needs meal choices per seat.

### Where should the parents sit?

At tables closest to the couple, typically one for each family. Seat them according to family dynamics.

### Should kids sit with parents?

Often yes, especially for younger children. A kids' table works for older children with an adult nearby.

### What if someone cancels late?

Leave a few open seats and update the chart. Tell the caterer and the rental company if the count changes.
