**Quick answer**

Good event signs are short, specific and placed where guests need them: a welcome sign at the entrance, directions at turns, a seating chart or table sign, a bar menu, restroom and coat check signs, and a gift or card table sign. Use plain words, large readable type, and place signs at eye level on easels. Write once, proof twice, and print a spare.

</div>

Guests do not read long signs. They glance, decide and move. When signs are missing or confusing, you answer the same questions all night: Where do I sit? Is the bar open? Where are the restrooms? A small set of clear signs reduces those questions and makes the event feel organized.

## Where signs help most

Think of the guest journey: arrival, check-in, coat drop, seating, food and drink, restrooms, entertainment, exit. Put a sign wherever a guest could hesitate. ## Create your signs in six steps

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>List the stops.</strong> Walk the guest path from the parking lot to the exit.</li>
<li><strong>Write the words.</strong> Short, kind and clear.</li>
<li><strong>Choose size and style.</strong> Match the room, with large lettering for distance.</li>
<li><strong>Proofread.</strong> Check spelling, dates and names twice.</li>
<li><strong>Print or order.</strong> Allow time, plus spares.</li>
<li><strong>Set up and test.</strong> Check sight lines and lighting from the guest's view.</li>
</ol>

## Design and accessibility basics

Use a plain, highly readable font, and avoid script for key info. Strong contrast, matte finishes and enough spacing help every guest, including older guests; see [planning an event for older guests](/blog/planning-an-event-for-older-guests). Use symbols for accessible routes and restrooms; see [ADA restroom considerations](/blog/ada-restroom-considerations-for-events). If you serve alcohol or food with allergens, label menus clearly.

For bars and menus, see [signature cocktail station ideas](/blog/signature-cocktail-station-ideas) and [bar placement floor plan](/blog/bar-placement-floor-plan). For neon and marquee options, see [marquee letters and neon signs rental](/blog/marquee-letters-and-neon-signs-rental).

## Tone and style tips

Match your wording to the event. A milestone birthday can be playful, a memorial calm and a corporate gala clean and direct. Use the same fonts and colors on every sign so they look like a set. Avoid jokes that need explanation, and keep names and dates correct. If your guests speak more than one language, add a short second line in that language on key signs, such as welcome, restrooms and food labels.

## Chicago-area notes

Outdoor Chicago events need signs that survive wind. Use weighted easels, clips and sturdy stands, and avoid flimsy foam boards on breezy days; see [chicago lakefront event wind planning](/blog/chicago-lakefront-event-wind-planning). Directional signs at the street help delivery drivers and guests find side entrances in dense neighborhoods. Check with your village, park district or building about rules for posting signs on public property. In winter, put signs inside entrances where they stay dry, and in rain use sleeves or frames.

## Frequently asked questions

### What signs do I need for an event?

A welcome sign, directions, a seating chart or table signs, a bar sign, restroom and coat check signs and a gift or card table sign cover most events.

### How big should event signs be?

Small signs work at close range, while entry signs and seating charts are often larger. Make text readable from a few feet away.

### What should a welcome sign say?

Keep it short: the occasion, the names and a friendly line. Avoid long paragraphs.

### Where should I place signs?

At eye level, at decision points, with good light and a clear view. Avoid blocking walkways.

### Do I need signs for outdoor events?

Yes, and use weighted stands. Check wind and rain protection.

### How do I make signs accessible?

Use high contrast, simple fonts, large type and symbols. Keep routes clear.

### Should I print extra copies?

Yes. A spare sign saves the day if one is damaged or misspelled.
