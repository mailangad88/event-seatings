**Quick answer**

Plan one cocktail table for every 6 to 8 guests at a standing reception. That is 13 to 17 tables for 100 guests and 25 to 34 for 200. Add a few seated spots for older guests, and leave 6 to 8 square feet per person for the whole room. Treat these as rules of thumb and check the venue's capacity for standing events.

</div>

Standing receptions are popular in Chicago for galas, fundraisers, corporate mixers and cocktail-style weddings. They save money on seating but are easy to misjudge. Too few tables and guests balance plates on the bar. Too many and the room feels like a furniture store.

## The ratio

**Tables = guests ÷ 6 to 8.**

<div class="table-wrap">

| Guests | Tables at 1 per 6 | Tables at 1 per 8 | Suggested seats |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 8 | 6 | 10 to 15 |
| 100 | 17 | 13 | 20 to 30 |
| 150 | 25 | 19 | 30 to 45 |
| 200 | 34 | 25 | 40 to 60 |
| 300 | 50 | 38 | 60 to 90 |

</div>

Use the lower table count for short mixers where guests mostly circulate. Use the higher count for events over two hours or with heavy appetizers, when guests need somewhere to put a plate.

## Space per guest

A standing reception uses less floor than a seated dinner, but guests need elbow room and a path to bars and food.

- **Standing only:** about 6 to 8 square feet per guest.
- **Standing with some seating:** about 8 to 10.
- **Seated dinner for comparison:** about 10 to 12 for rounds.

Check the venue's stated capacity for standing receptions. Many Chicago venues publish one number for seated and a higher one for standing, and fire marshals set the legal maximum.

## Table placement

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Start at the edges</strong> Tables along walls leave the center free.</li>
<li><strong>Cluster in 3s and 4s</strong> Groups help guests find each other.</li>
<li><strong>Keep tables 4 to 6 ft apart</strong> So guests can walk between them.</li>
<li><strong>Leave bar and food clear</strong> 10 ft around each station.</li>
<li><strong>Add a few seats</strong> Along the walls, for guests who need a rest.</li>
</ol>

## Mix in a seated lounge

At events longer than 2 hours, add a lounge area. A few [velvet dining chairs](/chairs/velvet-dining) or [bentwood chairs](/chairs/bentwood) in small groups keep older guests comfortable. See [lounge and specialty rentals](/blog/topic/lounge-and-specialty) for ideas.

## Highboys and linens

Highboys are typically 42 inches tall, with a 30 or 36-inch top. Cover them with a 120-inch round tied at the middle or spandex. Read [cocktail highboy vs lowboy tables](/blog/cocktail-highboy-vs-lowboy-tables) for the heights.

## Chicago timing

Winter receptions need a coat check or a coat rack zone, which eats floor space. Summer terraces and rooftops often limit guests by structure, so ask the building about capacity. A cocktail-style wedding often follows the same ratio for the first hour and then shifts to dinner tables.

## Worked example: a 120-guest fundraiser

A nonprofit holds a 120-guest cocktail reception at a loft near the Chicago River. The event runs two hours, with passed appetizers and two food stations.

1. Start with the ratio: 120 guests at one table per 7 gives about 17 highboys.
2. Add a lounge: 25 percent of guests, or 30 seats, in two small groupings with low tables.
3. Allow 7 square feet per guest: 840 square feet for standing space, plus bars and stations.
4. Mark 10 feet of clear space around each bar and food station.
5. Confirm the venue's standing capacity and the number of exits.

The result is 17 highboys, two lounge groupings and a clear center for circulation. If guests are likely to stay beyond two hours, bump the ratio to one table per 6 and add more seats.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Putting tables in rows.** Rows look like a classroom and block traffic. Stagger them.
- **Ignoring coat storage.** From November to March, a Chicago crowd arrives with coats. Plan a rack or checked room, away from the main walkway.
- **No trash points.** Standing guests collect plates and napkins. Put a bussing station or two on the edges.
- **Forgetting accessibility.** At least some tables should be at standard height with chairs.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many cocktail tables for 100 guests?

Plan 13 to 17 tables, using one per 6 to 8 guests. Add a handful of seats for older guests.

### How many people stand at a cocktail table?

Three to four at a 30-inch round and four to five at a 36-inch round.

### How many cocktail tables do I need for 50 guests?

Six to eight. Round up if the event runs longer than two hours.

### How much space does a standing reception need?

About 6 to 8 square feet per guest. Confirm the venue's maximum standing capacity.

### Should every guest have a seat at a cocktail reception?

No. A rule of thumb is seats for 20 to 30 percent of guests. Raise it if guests are older or the event is long.

### Can I use rounds instead of highboys?

Yes. Standard 30-inch tables with chairs make a seated cocktail hour. See [how many tables for 50 to 200 guests](/blog/how-many-tables-for-50-100-150-200-guests).
