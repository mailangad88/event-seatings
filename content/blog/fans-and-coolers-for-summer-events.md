**Quick answer**

Fans move air and make people feel cooler, while evaporative coolers add moisture to cool the air and work best in dry weather. Chicago summers are often humid, so fans do most of the work and misting or evaporative coolers help mainly on dry days. Plan one large pedestal or drum fan per 150 to 250 square feet of seated area in a tent, aimed across guests rather than at the food. Always check circuits first.

Summer in Chicago means a beautiful evening for a wedding, and sometimes a heat index that makes the tent feel like a greenhouse. Open sidewalls help, but tents trap heat, and formal clothing makes it worse. Fans are the quiet hero. This guide explains which cooling tools work, how to place them and what to ask for.

## Fans versus coolers versus air conditioning

<div class="table-wrap">

| Tool | How it helps | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Pedestal fan | Moves air across skin | Does not lower temperature |
| Drum or high-velocity fan | Moves more air, farther | Loud, can blow decor |
| Misting fan | Adds fine mist that cools by evaporation | Wets surfaces, less effective in humid air |
| Evaporative cooler (swamp cooler) | Cools air through evaporation | Works best in dry air, needs water and power |
| Portable air conditioner | Lowers air temperature | Needs heavy power and sealed spaces |
| Tent air conditioning (ducted) | Cools the whole tent | Large power and special setup |

</div>

The key concept is humidity. Evaporative cooling needs dry air to evaporate water. When the air is already humid, evaporation slows and the effect shrinks. Chicago summers vary, with some dry days and many sticky ones, so ask your supplier how a given cooler performs at the humidity you expect, and rely on fans as the baseline.

## How many and where

A reasonable starting point is one large fan per 150 to 250 square feet of seated area, more for crowded spaces. A 40 x 60-foot tent has 2,400 square feet, so a full dining tent may use 8 to 12 fans. These are rules of thumb. Ask your supplier for their guidance for your tent and weather.

Placement tips:

- **Aim fans across the room, not at faces.** A steady breeze at 6 feet feels good, while a gale at the dinner table dries food and blows napkins.
- **Raise fans off the floor.** A fan at head height cools more than one at ankle height.
- **Keep them away from candles, lightweight decor and food stations.**
- **Open the sidewalls on the upwind and downwind sides** so air moves through. A closed tent stays hot even with fans.
- **Leave walkways clear.** Tape or cover cords; see [cords on grass](/blog/cords-on-grass-outdoor-lighting-safety).

## Power

A pedestal fan draws modest power, and misting or evaporative units draw more. Check the label on each unit, add the watts, and look at [generator sizing](/blog/generator-size-for-events) and [event power needs](/blog/event-power-needs-checklist). Spread fans across several outlets, not one. Use outdoor-rated, appropriately thick cords as described in [extension cord safety](/blog/outdoor-extension-cord-safety-events).

## Heat and the schedule

Heat builds through the day, so the hottest part of a reception is often the first hour after dinner, when the tent is full and the sun is still on the fabric. Plan the hottest activities around that. Hold the cocktail hour in shade, serve dinner after the sun drops behind trees or buildings, and keep dancing for last, when the air has cooled. Tell your caterer and band so they can pace service and sets. If the forecast shows a heat advisory, add water stations, give older guests the shadiest seats and tell the venue so staff can watch for guests who feel unwell.

## Cooling without equipment

Equipment helps, but the layout and timing help more.

- Schedule the ceremony for late afternoon or evening instead of midday.
- Choose a shaded ceremony site or add a canopy.
- Offer chilled water at the entrance and ice in drinks.
- Offer paper fans or towels as favors that are useful.
- Keep seating compact but not crowded. Our [seating guides](/blog/topic/layouts-and-floor-plans) show how much space each guest needs.
- Use lighter linens and [chairs with breathable backs](/chairs).

## Chicago notes

The heat index can climb on lake-adjacent days when winds are calm. Rooftop and parking lot sites reflect heat. If your event is in a park, confirm with the park district whether fans, misting equipment and generators are allowed.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many fans do I need for a tent?

A starting guideline is one large fan per 150 to 250 square feet of seated area, but ask your supplier because tent height and weather change it.

### Do evaporative coolers work in Chicago?

They work best in dry air. On humid days, they do less, so rely on fans and open sidewalls.

### Is a misting fan worth it?

On dry, hot days, yes. On humid days, it mainly wets people and surfaces.

### Can I air-condition a tent?

Yes, with ducted units placed outside, but it needs significant power and a well-sealed tent. Ask a tent company for a quote.

### Where should fans be placed?

At head height, along the edges and aimed across the seating area. Keep them away from candles and food.

### Do fans need special cords?

Use outdoor-rated cords sized for the load, and keep them covered and off wet ground.
