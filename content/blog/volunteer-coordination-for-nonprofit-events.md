**Quick answer**

To coordinate volunteers, decide the roles you need, write a short role card for each, recruit 10 to 20 percent more people than you need, and set shifts of two to four hours. Hold a brief training, give every volunteer a lead and a contact number, feed them, and thank them within a week. Confirm everyone 48 hours before the event.

</div>

Volunteers are the engine of most nonprofit events, and they are also the first thing that falls apart when coordination is vague. People show up unsure of their job, stand around, or leave early because nobody told them where to go. A volunteer who feels useful and appreciated returns next year, so good coordination is both an event task and a long-term investment.

## Volunteer coordination rules of thumb

<div class="stat-grid">
<div><strong>10 to 20%</strong><span>Extra volunteers many coordinators recruit for no-shows</span></div>
<div><strong>2 to 4 hrs</strong><span>A comfortable shift length</span></div>
<div><strong>1 lead</strong><span>Per 8 to 10 volunteers</span></div>
<div><strong>48 hours</strong><span>Confirm shifts this far ahead</span></div>
<div><strong>1 page</strong><span>Role card for each position</span></div>
<div><strong>1 week</strong><span>Send thank-yous within this window</span></div>
</div>

These are common practices, not fixed rules. Your volunteers, the tasks and the risk level of the event all affect how many you need and how much training they should have.

## Define roles before you recruit

List every task, then group them into roles: greeters, registration, set-up crew, auction or merchandise staff, food and beverage helpers, marshals, runners and breakdown crew. For each role, write a one-page card with the purpose, tasks, the person to report to, the start and end time, what to wear, and what to do if there is a problem.

Match roles to the person. A shy volunteer may prefer a back-of-house task, while an outgoing person makes a great greeter. Be honest about physical requirements, such as lifting tables or standing for long periods, and offer seated roles where possible. Our [ADA accessible seating and pathways guide](/blog/ada-accessible-event-seating-and-pathways) helps you design welcoming spaces for volunteers and guests alike.

## Recruit, schedule and train

Recruit through your board, past volunteers, schools, faith groups, employers with volunteer programs and community boards. Ask for a specific commitment: "We need six greeters on Saturday, May 18, from 5 to 8 p.m." Use a sign-up tool or a shared sheet, and send a confirmation with the address, parking, role and contact number.

Training can be short: a 20 to 30 minute briefing before the shift, covering the event purpose, the schedule, a map, safety, and what to say to guests. For events with money, alcohol, minors or medical situations, ask your organization about extra training and policies. The [fundraising gala planning timeline](/blog/fundraising-gala-planning-timeline) and [silent auction planning guide](/blog/silent-auction-planning-guide) show where volunteers fit.

## Day-of management and appreciation

Set up a volunteer check-in table with name tags, a schedule, water and snacks. Have team leads who know the plan and carry a radio or phone. Rotate people out of hard roles, give breaks and feed them. Nothing sours volunteers faster than an unplanned, unfed shift.

Afterward, thank each person by name, share what the event raised or achieved, and invite them to the next one. A simple handwritten note or a small gathering means a lot. Track hours and feedback so you can improve, and keep contact information in your database in line with your organization's privacy policy.

## Handling problems on the day

Even a well-planned team has surprises. A volunteer may not show, someone may be unsure of a task, or a guest may be upset. Give leads authority to reassign people, and keep a short list of float volunteers who can fill gaps. Make sure everyone knows who to call for safety issues and where the first aid kit and restrooms are. Afterward, hold a five minute debrief with the leads while memories are fresh, and write down what to change for next time.

## Volunteers needed by event

This table gives rough starting counts for a few types of events. Adjust for complexity, safety needs and your venue.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Event | Guests | Volunteers (rough) | Key roles |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gala or dinner | 150 | 10 to 15 | Greeters, registration, auction, coat check |
| Silent auction | 150 | 8 to 12 | Table monitors, checkout, runners |
| Charity walk | 250 | 25 to 30 | Marshals, water, check-in, finish |
| Community festival | 500 | 40 to 60 | Gates, info, set-up, cleanup |
| School fundraiser | 200 | 15 to 20 | Check-in, food, games, tear-down |

</div>

Ask experienced volunteers or similar organizations how they staff their events and adjust your numbers over time.

## Coordination steps

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>List the tasks.</strong> Group them into roles with clear start and end times.</li>
<li><strong>Write role cards.</strong> One page per role.</li>
<li><strong>Recruit with specific asks.</strong> Dates, times and tasks.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm and brief.</strong> Send details 48 hours ahead and hold a short training.</li>
<li><strong>Manage the day.</strong> Check-in table, leads, breaks and food.</li>
<li><strong>Thank and follow up.</strong> Notes, results and an invitation back.</li>
</ol>

## Chicago-area notes

Chicago-area volunteers often travel by train or car, so give clear transit and parking instructions and consider a shift end time that fits train schedules. In cold months, provide a warm space for volunteers working outdoors, and in hot months, give water and shade. Many schools and employers offer volunteer hours or service days, so ask about partnerships. For events in parks, ask the park district about volunteer requirements, and for events involving minors, check your organization's policies and local rules. ## Frequently asked questions

### How many volunteers do I need for an event?

It depends on the event. A common approach is to list the tasks, estimate how many people each needs and recruit 10 to 20 percent extra for no-shows.

### How do I recruit volunteers?

Ask your board, past volunteers, schools, faith groups and employers. Make a specific ask with dates, times and tasks.

### How long should a volunteer shift be?

Two to four hours is comfortable for most people. Longer shifts need breaks and meals.

### What training do volunteers need?

A short briefing on the event purpose, schedule, role, safety and who to call. Some events need extra training.

### How do I keep volunteers from not showing up?

Confirm 48 hours before, send reminders, and recruit extra. Make the role clear and the shift easy to attend.

### Should I feed volunteers?

Yes. Water, snacks and a meal for longer shifts show appreciation and keep energy up.

### How do I thank volunteers?

Thank them by name within a week, share the event results, and invite them back. A handwritten note goes a long way.
