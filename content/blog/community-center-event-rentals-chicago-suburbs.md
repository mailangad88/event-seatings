**Quick answer**

Community centers and park district field houses are budget-friendly rooms with strict house rules. Ask for capacity, table and chair inventory, kitchen access, alcohol and decoration rules, setup and cleanup time and the cancellation policy. Then rent what is missing: more or nicer chairs, linens, dinnerware, serving equipment and lighting.

</div>

Community centers, field houses, recreation centers and fraternal halls are workhorses of the Chicago area. They host graduations, quinceañeras, baby showers, memorial gatherings and fundraisers at a price many commercial venues cannot match. The trade-off is rules and a plain room. This guide helps you plan both.

## What these rooms usually look like

Expect a multipurpose room, sometimes a gym, with folding tables and stackable chairs, a kitchenette or warming kitchen, nearby restrooms and a large parking lot. In Chicago, the Park District's site says indoor fieldhouse rooms are booked through the specific park, and that unique indoor spaces for weddings or organizational events are handled through a Special Event Venue permit. In the suburbs, the park district, village or township sets its own booking system. Verify everything with the facility.

## House rules to expect

Decoration rules are the most common surprise. Many facilities ban tape and nails on walls and open flame. Alcohol policies vary from banned to allowed with a licensed server and insurance. Some require a facility staff member present, which may add a fee. Setup and teardown windows are often tight, so make sure the rental hours include them. Ask whether outside rental companies may deliver, and when.

## Church halls

A church hall is another common venue, with its own rules about alcohol, music and use of the sanctuary. Many hosts of church-related events also want decor guidance; see [decor for church wedding receptions](/blog/decor-for-church-wedding-receptions). We cannot speak for any specific parish, so ask the parish office.

## Budget tips that work

The best savings come from spending in the right places. Keep the facility's tables if they are in good shape and cover them with cloths. Rent chairs only if the facility's chairs are uncomfortable or unattractive, or if you need more than they have. Bring light, not just decor: warm lamps and string lights change a fluorescent room more than expensive centerpieces. Use the kitchen for warming and cold storage, and rent serving equipment only for what the kitchen lacks.

## Accessibility and guest flow

Public buildings are often well set up for accessibility, but check anyway. Ask about ramps, doors, restroom access and parking close to the entrance. Place tables so a wheelchair or walker can reach every seat, and leave wide aisles to the restrooms. Our [ADA restroom considerations guide](/blog/ada-restroom-considerations-for-events) explains why planning matters.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does it cost to rent a community center?

Fees vary by city, park district and room. Ask the facility for its price list; we don't quote venue fees.

### Do community centers provide tables and chairs?

Most have folding tables and stackable chairs. Ask for counts and condition.

### Can I serve alcohol at a community center?

Policies vary from banned to allowed with conditions. Ask the facility.

### Can I decorate a community center room?

Usually, with limits. Many ban tape, nails and open flame.

### How many people fit in a community center room?

Use the posted capacity. As a rule of thumb, ten 60-inch rounds seat about 80.

### Can an outside rental company deliver to a community center?

Often yes, but confirm delivery windows and access.

### What should I rent to upgrade a community center event?

Better chairs, floor-length linens, warm lighting and serving equipment make the biggest difference.
