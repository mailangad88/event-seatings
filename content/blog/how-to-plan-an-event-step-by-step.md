**Quick answer**

Plan an event in this order: set the purpose and guest count, set a budget, pick a date, choose a venue, book the biggest vendors, send invitations, confirm every detail a week out, then run the day from a written timeline. Doing the decisions in that order keeps you from rebooking things later. Most events need six to twelve weeks, and larger ones need more.

</div>

Most people who plan their first event do not fail at any one task. They fail at the order of tasks. They pick a caterer before they know the headcount, or fall for a venue before checking the budget, and then spend weeks undoing choices. This guide gives you the sequence that works for a backyard birthday, a wedding reception or a company dinner, with the reasons behind each step so you can adjust it for your own event.

## Step 1: Decide what the event is for

Write one sentence that finishes "This event exists so that...". A graduation open house exists so that friends and relatives can congratulate one person. A company dinner exists so that a team feels appreciated. That sentence helps you say no to extras later. It also tells you the right guest count, because the purpose decides who must be there.

Then write down three numbers: a rough guest count, a total budget and a target date range. These three decide almost everything else, so do not skip them even if they are estimates.

## Step 2: Build the budget before you shop

Money is easiest to manage when you divide it on day one. Our guide on [how to make an event budget](/blog/how-to-make-an-event-budget) shows a split you can copy. Set aside a cushion for the things nobody remembers, such as extra ice, tips, delivery fees and a last-minute chair count change.

## Step 3: Pick the date and the place together

Date and venue depend on each other. A popular room may only have a few open Saturdays, and a backyard needs a weather plan. Read [how to choose an event date](/blog/how-to-choose-an-event-date) before you commit, and ask any venue what is included so you only rent what is missing. Our list of [questions to ask a venue about rentals](/blog/questions-to-ask-venue-about-rentals) is a good start.

## Step 4: Build the guest list

A guest list drives food, seating and rentals. Draft it early, even if it is long, then trim. [How to make a guest list for any event](/blog/how-to-make-a-guest-list-for-any-event) walks through tiers so cuts feel fair instead of personal.

## Step 5: Book the vendors that sell out first

Book in order of scarcity. Venues and caterers usually go first, then photographers, DJs or bands, then rentals, then flowers and smaller items. [How to hire event vendors](/blog/how-to-hire-event-vendors) covers what to ask and what to get in writing. Rentals such as chairs, tables, linens and tents should be requested once you have a headcount. A [rental quote](/blog/how-event-rental-quotes-work) is easier to build when your numbers are firm.

## Step 6: Send invitations and track replies

Give guests enough notice, with a clear reply date that sits a few days before your caterer's deadline. Track every answer in one place. See [how to track RSVPs](/blog/how-to-track-rsvps) for a system that does not rely on your memory.

## Step 7: Build the day-of plan

Write a timeline that includes setup, arrivals, food, any program, cleanup and pickups. Add names beside each item. The [four-hour party timeline](/blog/four-hour-party-timeline) is a good model. Print it, and give a copy to your most reliable helper.

## Chicago-area notes

Chicago weather shapes the plan. Spring and fall can swing from sunny to cold and wet within days, and summer storms arrive fast, so any outdoor event needs a backup plan. Look at [rain on event day in Chicago](/blog/rain-on-event-day-chicago) before you decide on a tent. Also ask early about parking, street cleaning and delivery access, because a rental truck needs a place to stop. For parks, tents and block parties, permit rules differ between the city, park districts and individual villages, so check with your village or park district well ahead. Our [Chicago special event permit guide](/blog/chicago-special-event-permit-guide) explains the general process.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I start planning an event?

Start by writing the purpose of the event, then a rough guest count, a total budget and a date range. Those three numbers drive every other choice. Once they are set, you can look at venues and vendors with confidence.

### How far in advance should I plan an event?

Many planners allow six to twelve weeks for a mid-size party and several months for a large event or a wedding. Small gatherings can come together in a few weeks. Peak seasons book earlier, so start sooner for popular dates.

### What is the first thing to do when planning an event?

Decide why you are hosting and who needs to be there. That tells you the guest count and the style. Then set a budget, because every vendor quote will be measured against it.

### What should I book first for an event?

Book the items that sell out first, usually the venue and the caterer. Photographers, entertainment and rentals follow. Smaller items such as favors and flowers can wait until the main pieces are locked.

### How do I plan an event with no experience?

Use a checklist and a timeline, and ask each vendor what comes next. A simple planning kit keeps tasks, dates and costs together. Do not be afraid to ask a rental or catering company how other hosts usually handle a detail.

### How much should I budget for an event?

It depends on guest count, venue, food and season, so get several quotes before you fix a number. Many planners keep a cushion of around ten to fifteen percent for surprises. Our budget guide shows a typical split.

### Do I need an event planner?

Not always. Small and simple events are well handled by a host with a good checklist. A planner or coordinator helps when the guest list is large, many vendors are involved or you cannot be present to manage the day.
