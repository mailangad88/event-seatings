**Quick answer**

For a panel, seat the moderator on one end and panelists in a gentle curve or straight line, with 30 to 36 inches between chairs and about 4 feet of depth. Use stools or armchairs, with or without a low table, on a stage 18 to 24 inches high if the audience exceeds 75. Seat the audience in theater rows or cabaret rounds facing the center.

</div>

A panel looks simple: four chairs, a mic and a moderator. In practice, bad panel seating makes people squint at profiles, hides the panelists' faces, and forces them to twist to speak to each other. The fix is a handful of measurements and one decision about whether to use a table. This guide covers panel layouts for a Chicago hotel ballroom, an office town hall, a university auditorium or a nonprofit forum.

## Chairs and spacing

Panelists sit for 45 to 90 minutes, so avoid backless stools unless the panel is short. Use [bentwood chairs](/chairs/bentwood), [wishbone chairs](/chairs/wishbone) or [velvet chairs](/chairs/velvet-dining) for comfort and style. Space them 30 to 36 inches apart center to center; mic cables and water glasses need room. Leave 4 feet of depth for the group, and 6 feet between the stage edge and the first audience row.

## The table question

A table gives panelists a place for water, notes and a mic, but it also hides body language and blocks the audience's view. If the panel needs notes or laptops, use a narrow 18-inch table. If it's a conversation, skip the table and use a small side table between every two chairs for a glass. A skirted table at the front helps if panelists bring materials.

## Where the moderator sits

Place the moderator on one end, angled slightly toward the panel, so they can look at both the panelists and the audience. A moderator in the center forces everyone to look past them. For a podium-style event, the moderator may stand at a lectern, but that adds distance. Water for each panelist, a notepad and a small clock or timer should be within reach.

## Audience seating

For 20 to 75 guests, theater rows or a semicircle fit well. For 100 or more, add a raised stage and theater seating; see [how many chairs fit in theater seating](/blog/how-many-chairs-fit-theater-seating). For meal-and-panel events, use rounds with open seats toward the stage; see [conference seating styles](/blog/conference-seating-styles-theater-classroom-banquet-u-shape).

## Mics, lighting and screens

Give each panelist a lavalier or handheld mic, and test every one. Light the stage so faces are clear, not backlit by a bright screen. Put a screen at the side or behind the panel and keep text large enough to read from the back row. For a hybrid panel, put a camera at the back and a screen facing the panel so remote participants appear; see [hybrid event AV and seating](/blog/hybrid-event-av-and-seating).

## A pre-event run-through

Do a 10-minute rehearsal on the actual stage. Have panelists sit, check that each can see the moderator, the audience and the clock, and watch for glare on screens. Adjust chair angles by a few degrees until everyone can turn to speak without twisting more than 45 degrees. Mark chair positions with a small piece of tape on the stage floor, since chairs wander during a long panel. Place a stool or small table near the stage for the moderator's notes and a pitcher of water. Leave a clear path from the wings so panelists can enter and exit without stepping over cables.

## Choosing between stools and chairs

High stools lift panelists to eye level with an audience in a large room, which helps when the stage is low. But stools are tiring after 45 minutes and awkward for anyone in a skirt or with a bad back. Armchairs and bentwood chairs give support, and a low lounge chair feels relaxed but puts the panelist's face below the audience's sightline. If the stage is 24 inches high and the panel is under 60 minutes, stools are fine; otherwise choose chairs with backs.

## Accessibility

Plan accessible seating for panelists and audience, a ramp or lift to the stage, and a place for a sign language interpreter in the sightline of the audience members who need it. Ask panelists whether they need armrests or a higher seat.

## Frequently asked questions

### How far apart should panelists sit?

30 to 36 inches center to center. Closer feels cramped and farther makes it hard to hear each other.

### Should panelists have a table?

Only if they need notes or laptops. For conversation, skip the table and use small side tables for water.

### Where does the moderator sit?

At one end, angled toward the panel, so they can see both panelists and the audience.

### How big should a panel stage be?

About 4 feet deep and 4 to 5 feet per panelist wide, plus space for the moderator.

### What chairs work best for a panel?

Chairs with a back and a firm seat, such as bentwood or velvet. Avoid low, soft sofas that make people slouch.

### How should I seat the audience?

In theater rows or rounds that face the stage. Leave a center aisle for question mics.
