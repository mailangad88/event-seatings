**Quick answer**

A run of show is a minute-by-minute schedule of what happens, who does it and what each person needs, from load-in to teardown. Write it by listing every moment, assigning a time, an owner and a cue, then adding buffers. Share one version with all vendors and staff, print copies for the day, and keep one person in charge of changes.

</div>

On event day, nobody has time to ask what happens next. The caterer wants to know when to serve, the DJ wants to know when to stop, the speaker wants to know where to stand. A run of show answers all of it in one document. Without it, decisions get made on the fly, usually by whoever is loudest, and small delays ripple into the evening.

## Run of show rules of thumb

<div class="stat-grid">
<div><strong>5 to 10 min</strong><span>Buffer many planners add between major segments</span></div>
<div><strong>1 owner</strong><span>Every line has a person responsible</span></div>
<div><strong>1 version</strong><span>Everyone works from the latest copy</span></div>
<div><strong>1 week</strong><span>Share a draft with vendors this far ahead</span></div>
<div><strong>Load-in</strong><span>Include setup and teardown, not just the program</span></div>
<div><strong>Cues</strong><span>Write what triggers each moment</span></div>
</div>

These are habits professionals commonly use, not rules. Scale the detail to your event, from a one-page sheet for a dinner to a multi-tab file for a conference.

## What a run of show includes

A good run of show covers the entire day, not only the program. Start with vendor arrivals and load-in, then room setup, rehearsal, doors, the program segments, meals, breaks, transitions, departures and teardown. For each line, include the start time, duration, what happens, who is responsible, and any tech or cues, such as lights, music or a slide change.

It is different from an agenda. An agenda tells guests what to expect. A run of show tells the team what to do. Many events have both, and the agenda is a trimmed version of the run of show.

## How to write it step by step

Begin with fixed points: doors, dinner service, the keynote or ceremony, and the end time. Then work backward and forward from them, filling in setup, rehearsals and teardown. Next, add detail on each segment. Who speaks, with what notes, with which microphone? Who cues the music? Where do people move?

Interview each vendor about their needs: how long to set up, when to eat, when to power up. Add travel times between rooms, and add buffers. People always take longer to move than planned. Our [corporate event planning checklist](/blog/corporate-event-planning-checklist) and [conference planning timeline](/blog/conference-planning-timeline) explain how the run of show fits into the larger plan.

## Sharing and managing changes

Create one master copy, ideally a shared document or sheet, and tell everyone where it is. Print copies for the day and keep a clean master with the event lead. Version it with a date and time so no one works from an old copy. During the event, one person calls cues and approves changes, and everyone else sends requests to that person.

Hold a short briefing before doors open, covering the schedule, problem points and contacts. Give vendors a one-page version with only their lines. For a gala or program with volunteers, see [volunteer coordination](/blog/volunteer-coordination-for-nonprofit-events), which includes role cards.

## Writing steps

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>List fixed points.</strong> Doors, meal, main moment and end time.</li>
<li><strong>Fill in setup and teardown.</strong> Add load-in, rehearsals and breakdown windows.</li>
<li><strong>Detail each segment.</strong> Name the owner, the cue and the equipment.</li>
<li><strong>Add buffers.</strong> Pad transitions and moves between rooms.</li>
<li><strong>Share a draft.</strong> Collect corrections from vendors and speakers.</li>
<li><strong>Finalize and print.</strong> One master, dated, and a short briefing before doors.</li>
</ol>

## Chicago-area notes

In Chicago, setup and breakdown windows are often tighter than expected, especially at downtown buildings with loading docks and freight elevators. Put the dock schedule, parking instructions and building contact on the run of show, and read the [Chicago loading zone guide](/blog/chicago-event-delivery-parking-loading-zones). Weather matters for outdoor events: add a decision point, such as "3:00 p.m. weather call," with a named person who decides whether to move indoors. For suburban venues, check any noise or end time limits with your village or the venue and put them on the schedule.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a run of show?

It is a detailed schedule of everything that happens at an event, with times, owners and cues, from load-in to teardown.

### How is a run of show different from an agenda?

An agenda is for guests and lists the program. A run of show is for the team and includes setup, vendor tasks, cues and backup plans.

### How do I write a run of show?

List fixed points, add setup and teardown, detail each segment with an owner and cue, add buffers, share a draft and finalize.

### Who should get a copy of the run of show?

Event leads, vendors, speakers and volunteers, often in versions that show only their parts. Keep one master copy.

### How detailed should a run of show be?

Detailed enough that someone could run the event if the planner stepped away. For a simple dinner, one page is fine. For a conference, it can be many pages.

### How much buffer time should I include?

Many planners add five to ten minutes between major segments. Add more where people need to move between rooms.

### What do I do if the event runs behind?

Pick segments you can shorten ahead of time, such as remarks or breaks, and give one person authority to make the call.
