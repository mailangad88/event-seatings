**Quick answer**

Set a firm ceiling based on your venue and budget, then sort every name into three tiers: must invite, would love to invite and nice to have. Fill the list from the top tier down and stop at the ceiling. Apply one rule to everyone in a category, such as work friends or plus-ones, so decisions feel fair instead of personal.

</div>

Almost every couple writes a first draft that is far larger than the venue or budget allows. The hard part is not math. It is deciding who goes without feeling like you are ranking people you love. The best way through is to separate the decision from the person: use categories and rules, not individual judgments.

## Start with a ceiling, not a list

Pick the maximum number first. The ceiling comes from the venue capacity, the catering budget and how the day will feel. A room that holds 150 comfortably may feel empty at 70 and cramped at 180. Check the capacity with your venue and ask about rentals needed for each additional table. See [wedding rentals by guest count](/blog/wedding-reception-rentals-by-guest-count) for how the equipment grows with the count.

Then split the ceiling among the people hosting. A common approach is to divide it between the couple and each set of parents, with a shared share for friends. Everyone can see where the space goes.

## Sort into tiers

Write down everyone, then sort them. Tier one is immediate family and the friends who would be hurt if they were left out. Tier two is extended family and close friends you see regularly. Tier three is everyone else: coworkers, neighbors, distant relatives, and the friends you are still deciding about.

Fill the guest list from tier one and add tier two until you reach the ceiling. Do not move into tier three unless you have room. If you do have room, wait for early declines to send a second wave of invitations, which works best if you send the first wave early enough.

## Use rules, not exceptions

A rule is easier to explain than a decision. Examples: we are only inviting people we have seen in the last two years, we are not inviting coworkers, or plus-ones are for married or engaged partners. Whatever you pick, apply it to everyone in the group. The moment you make one exception, the rest will notice.

Children deserve a clear policy too. An adults-only wedding is fine if the invitation says so plainly, and a small kids' area works for family weddings. See [kids table wedding ideas](/blog/kids-table-wedding-ideas) if you plan to include children.

## Who to invite first, and when to cut

This table shows how many couples sort common groups. Your family and friend situation will differ, so adapt it.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Group | Typical tier | Common rule to apply |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate family | 1 | Always invited |
| Grandparents, aunts, uncles | 1 or 2 | Invite all or none by side |
| Close friends | 1 or 2 | People you talk to monthly |
| Cousins | 2 or 3 | Invite all first cousins or none |
| Coworkers | 3 | Invite none, or only your team |
| Parents' friends | 2 or 3 | Cap by number per family |
| Plus-ones | 2 or 3 | Married, engaged or long-term |

</div>

If you still have too many names after applying the rules, reduce the ceiling's categories rather than removing individuals. It is easier to say no to a group than to a person.

## A calm way to cut the list

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Set the ceiling together.</strong> Use venue capacity and the budget.</li>
<li><strong>Divide space among hosts.</strong> Couple, each family and shared friends.</li>
<li><strong>Sort into three tiers.</strong> Decide categories before names.</li>
<li><strong>Write your rules down.</strong> Keep them short and consistent.</li>
<li><strong>Send in waves if needed.</strong> First wave to tiers one and two; second to tier three as spots open.</li>
</ol>

## What to say when someone asks why they were not invited

You do not owe anyone a detailed explanation, but a short, kind script helps. Something like: we are keeping the day very small because of the venue, and we wish we could include everyone. Say it the same way to everyone, and avoid giving reasons that can be argued with, such as the budget. Capacity is a reason that is hard to debate.

If a family member pushes, remind them of the shared list and the rule that applies to everyone. Offer a different way to celebrate, such as a casual get-together after the wedding, a brunch or a toast at a later date. Many couples find that people who are invited to a smaller event afterward feel included without needing to be at the main day.

## Chicago-area notes

Chicago-area families are often large, with extended relatives across many suburbs, and many couples have friends who live out of state. If your list includes people who must travel, send save-the-dates earlier and ask about a [hotel room block](/blog/hotel-room-blocks-for-wedding-guests). Some Chicago venues have firm fire-code capacities that include staff, vendors and the dance floor, so ask for the legal maximum, not only the seating capacity. Winter weather can reduce attendance, so many planners keep a short waiting list when the date is between December and March.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I cut my wedding guest list without offending anyone?

Use categories and rules instead of individual decisions. For example, no coworkers, only first cousins or adults only. When everyone in a group is treated the same, there is less room for hurt feelings.

### What is a fair way to split the guest list with parents?

A common approach is to give the couple half the spots and divide the rest between the two sets of parents, sometimes with a shared pool for family friends. Decide the split before anyone writes a list.

### Do I have to invite coworkers to my wedding?

No. Many couples invite none or only the coworkers they socialize with outside work. If you invite some, apply a clear rule such as those on your own team.

### Should I allow plus-ones?

Plus-ones are a budget decision. Many couples allow them for married, engaged or long-term partners and skip them for single guests. Whatever you choose, apply it to everyone.

### Can I have an adults-only wedding?

Yes, and it is common. State it clearly on the invitation and your wedding website, and consider offering a babysitting suggestion for out-of-town families.

### How many people decline a wedding invitation?

It varies by group and travel distance. Local friends usually attend at higher rates, and distant guests decline more. Some couples invite a second wave if early declines come in, so keep a waiting list.

### What if I am still over my limit?

Lower the ceiling's categories, not individuals, or consider a bigger venue plus a higher budget. A small ceremony with a larger party later is another option. See [small wedding planning under 50 guests](/blog/small-wedding-planning-under-50-guests).
