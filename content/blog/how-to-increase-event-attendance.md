**Quick answer**

To increase event attendance, make the reason to come specific, invite people early through the channels they actually read, make registration take under a minute, and send reminders at a week, a day and the morning of. Pick a date and time that fits your audience, remove barriers like parking and cost uncertainty, and ask partners and speakers to share. Track registrations weekly so you can adjust.

</div>

Low turnout is rarely about the event itself. More often, people didn't hear about it, didn't know why it mattered, or hit a small obstacle at the last step: a confusing form, a parking worry, a weeknight conflict. Fixing attendance means working through the path from first message to walking in the door, and removing friction at each step.

These are common practices, not guarantees. Free events usually see more no-shows than paid ones, and every audience differs, so track your own numbers over time.

## Give people a reason to come

Start with a one-sentence reason that speaks to the audience: what they will learn, who they will meet or what they will help achieve. Put that sentence first in every message. Include practical details people need to decide, such as date, time, location, cost, who it is for, and what is included.

Pick a date and time with the audience in mind. Check school calendars, holidays, big local events and transit schedules. For professionals, midweek mornings or lunch often beat Friday evenings. For families and community events, weekends and early evenings usually work better. Ask a few members of your audience before you commit.

## Reach people where they are

Use two or three channels, not just one. Email to your own list, social posts, partner newsletters, personal invitations, flyers in relevant places and a simple web page work together. Ask speakers, sponsors and partners to share, and give them ready-to-use text and images so it is easy.

Personal outreach is powerful. A short note from a leader or a friend often beats a mass email. For internal events, ask managers to mention the event in team meetings. For community events, ask neighborhood groups. Our [event sponsorship packages guide](/blog/event-sponsorship-packages-guide) explains how sponsors can help amplify your reach.

## Remove friction and keep momentum

Make registration simple: few fields, mobile friendly, and a confirmation email with calendar link, address, parking and what to expect. Collect dietary needs at the same time. Send reminders at a week, a day and the morning of with the key details. Our [registration and check-in setup guide](/blog/event-registration-and-check-in-setup) covers the day-of side.

Think about barriers. Is parking clear and free? Is the location transit friendly? Is childcare needed? Are there accessibility issues? Our [ADA accessible seating guide](/blog/ada-accessible-event-seating-and-pathways) lists simple checks. A comfortable setup also helps, since people tell others when an event felt welcoming. See [how many chairs you need](/blog/how-many-chairs-do-i-need) to avoid a crowded or empty-looking room.

If few people open your messages, work on reach and subject lines. If many open but few register, work on the reason to come and the form. If many register but few attend, work on reminders and barriers.

## Timeline for outreach

This table maps outreach to the weeks before an event. Adjust the dates for your size and audience.

<div class="table-wrap">

| When | Action | Channel |
|---|---|---|
| 6 weeks out | Save the date and web page live | Email, social, partners |
| 4 weeks out | Formal invitation, registration open | Email, personal notes |
| 2 weeks out | Speaker or highlight announcement | Social, newsletter |
| 1 week out | Reminder with logistics | Email, text if permitted |
| 1 day out | Reminder with parking and arrival | Email, text if permitted |
| Morning of | Final nudge and weather note | Email, social |

After the event, thank attendees and ask for feedback. That also builds your list for next time.

## Steps to boost attendance

<ol class="flow">

<li><strong>Simplify registration.</strong> Short form, mobile friendly, clear confirmation.</li>

## Chicago-area notes

In Chicago, weather and transit shape attendance. A winter evening can cut turnout if snow is forecast, so send a weather note and consider a remote option. In summer, festivals and street closures can block routes, so check the city and neighborhood calendars. Suburban events benefit from clear parking details, and city events from transit directions. Mention nearby train or bus lines and any parking garages. For outdoor events, say what the plan is if it rains, and point to a tent or indoor backup. Check with the park district or village if you need to post signs or banners.

## Frequently asked questions

### How can I increase attendance at my event?

Give a clear reason to come, invite early through several channels, simplify registration, send reminders and remove barriers like parking and unclear costs.

### How far in advance should I send invitations?

Four to six weeks is common for a mid-sized event. Larger or travel events need more time.

### How many reminders should I send?

Many planners send three: a week before, a day before and the morning of. Keep each short and practical.

### Why do people register but not show up?

Common causes are schedule changes, forgetting, weather and low commitment for free events. Reminders and a clear value help.

### Does a free event get lower attendance?

Free events often have more no-shows than paid events. Reminders, personal outreach and a small commitment can improve show rates.

### What is the best day and time for an event?

It depends on your audience. Ask a few of them and check local calendars. Midweek lunch suits many professionals, while weekends suit families.

### How do I know if my promotion is working?

Track registrations weekly against your goal and note which channels send people. Adjust where you spend time.
