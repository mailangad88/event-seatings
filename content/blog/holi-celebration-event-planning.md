## What Holi is and how it varies

Holi is a Hindu spring festival, widely celebrated across India and the diaspora, and marked in many communities by throwing colored powder (gulal), playing music and sharing food. The night before, some communities light a bonfire (Holika Dahan) that symbolizes good winning over evil. The day itself is Rangwali Holi or Dhulandi, the festival of colors. Practices vary: some families focus on prayer and sweets, some host big public color events, and in some places the celebration lasts several days.

Because the day is joyful and physical, it helps to be clear in your invitation about what to expect: who will get colored, what to wear, and where the quiet areas are. Always respect consent. Not everyone wants to be covered in color, and it is polite to ask before applying.

## Colors and safety

Use powder labeled as skin safe, non-toxic and made for Holi or cosmetic use. Some cheap powders contain irritants. Avoid throwing near faces, eyes and mouths, and keep powder away from anyone with asthma or breathing sensitivities. Provide eye rinse water and a place to sit. Encourage guests to apply oil or lotion on skin and hair beforehand and wear clothes they do not mind losing. Consider glasses or sunglasses and masks for those who want them.

Choose a location that allows color. Many parks, venues and rental homes ban powder, so ask in writing. Lawns recover, but light stone, siding and pale fabric can stain. Lay tarps near furniture, and keep tents and linens away from the color zone. If you must use rentals, use darker linens or plan to keep tables out of range, and read the rental company's policy on stains, as in [linen stain and damage policies](/blog/linen-stain-and-damage-policies).

Food should be well away from color. Use covered dishes, and set a hand wash station before the food line.

<li><strong>Open the color zone.</strong> Have staff or volunteers keep an eye on safety and water.</li>
<li><strong>Take a food break.</strong> Serve snacks and sweets such as gujiya, thandai or chaat, with allergy labels.</li>
<li><strong>Rinse and rest.</strong> Guests wash up, dry off and sit in the quiet area.</li>
<li><strong>Close with cleanup.</strong> Collect trash, rinse tarps and check the space for stains.</li>
</ol>

## Cleanup and neighbors

Bring buckets, brooms, hoses (if allowed) and trash bags. Color powder can blow, so choose a calm day and keep the area away from neighbors' cars and windows. Dispose of powder as trash and avoid washing large amounts into storm drains. Give guests a bag for their colored clothes. Offer a change area, since colored guests will not want to sit in a car seat. Tell neighbors about the event, and mention the hours and the music.

## Alternatives for a smaller or indoor event

If you cannot use powder, consider a "dry" Holi with colored scarves, flower petals, face paint or a color photo wall. Many schools and offices hold color-free celebrations with Indian sweets, music and dance. This is a good option for workplaces, school events and mixed groups, and it avoids stains and breathing concerns.

## Chicago-area notes

Holi falls in March in most years, when Chicago weather is cold, wet and unpredictable. Many local celebrations are held on a weekend afternoon in late March or April, or move indoors. If you want an outdoor event, plan for a tent with sidewalls and heaters, and see [tent heater vs. patio heater](/blog/tent-heater-vs-patio-heater). Check with your park district or village before using powder in a public park, because many have rules about it and about permits for group events. Learn about noise ordinances and parking. Mud and melting snow can also be a factor, so consider flooring for walkways; see [tent flooring and subfloors](/blog/tent-flooring-and-subfloors).

## Frequently asked questions

### When is Holi celebrated?

Holi is held in spring, usually in March, on a date that follows the Hindu lunar calendar and changes each year. Check a current calendar, and note that some communities celebrate on different days.

### How much color powder do I need for a Holi party?

A common starting point is 1 to 2 ounces per guest, with more for a big crowd that throws a lot. Order extra, because it is easy to run out and hard to find at the last minute.

### Is Holi color safe?

Look for powder labeled skin safe and non-toxic. Avoid eyes and mouths, and keep it away from people with asthma or allergies. Provide water for rinsing.

### Can I host Holi in a public park?

Rules vary. Many parks restrict powder or require a permit for group events. Check with your park district or village in advance and get permission in writing.

### Will color stain rental equipment?

It can. Colored powder may stain linens, chairs and tents. Keep rentals out of the color zone, and ask your rental company about their policy before you order.

### What do you wear to a Holi party?

Wear white or old clothes you do not mind staining, plus comfortable shoes. Oil on skin and hair helps. Bring a change of clothes.

### What food is served at Holi?

Common treats include gujiya, thandai, chaat, samosas and other sweets and snacks, but it varies by region. Label allergens and keep food away from the color area.

### Can I have a Holi party indoors?

Yes, with dry color alternatives such as flower petals, colored scarves or face paint, and a venue that allows it. Check floors and carpet before you begin.
