**Quick answer**

Dry hire means you rent the bar, glasses and equipment and supply the alcohol, mixers and staff yourself. Full service means a bar company brings bartenders, equipment and often the stock. Dry hire gives you control and a smaller bill for equipment, but you take on the work, the venue approval and the liability. Full service costs more but shifts staffing, service and often insurance to a licensed company.

</div>

"Dry hire" is borrowed from the UK, where it means renting equipment without staff. In the Chicago area, you will see it called bar rental only, equipment-only or self-serve bar. The choice between it and full service is mostly about who carries the risk and the workload on the day.

**Dry hire** usually includes a bar front, back bar, tables, linens, glassware, ice tubs and delivery. You buy the alcohol, find bartenders and provide insurance if the venue asks.

**Full service** typically includes bartenders, the bar setup, glasses, ice and sometimes alcohol, mixers and garnish. It may include a license or insurance, and often breakdown and cleanup.

**Hybrid.** A common middle path is to hire licensed bartenders, who bring their own tools, and rent bar furniture and glassware from a rental company. Ask whether the bar company wants to use your glassware.

## When dry hire works

- A backyard party where the venue is your own property
- A small gathering with a few dozen guests
- A beer and wine only event where self-serve is simple. See [beer and wine only bar setup](/blog/beer-and-wine-only-bar-setup).
- An event run by an organization that already holds the correct licenses

## When full service is the safer choice

- Weddings and galas over 75 guests
- Any venue that requires licensed or insured bartenders
- Public spaces, parks and forest preserves where alcohol is tightly regulated
- Events where you sell tickets that include drinks
- Hosts who do not want to be responsible for cutting off a guest

Read the [Illinois alcohol rules for private events](/blog/illinois-alcohol-rules-private-events) before you choose, since the venue and city often decide for you.

</ol>

## A Chicago-area reality check

Many hotels, banquet halls and downtown event spaces have their own liquor license and a bar program, which means a dry hire setup is simply not an option there. Barns, lofts, backyards and private homes are more flexible, but even those may have a venue contract that names approved bar vendors. The most common mistake is to rent the bar first and ask the venue second. Reverse that order: get the venue's written bar policy, then choose dry hire, hybrid or full service, then rent the gear.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does dry hire mean for a bar?

Dry hire means renting equipment only, such as bar fronts and glassware, without bartenders or alcohol. You supply the staff and the drinks.

### Is dry hire cheaper than full service?

Usually less on paper, because you do not pay for staff. But you take on the cost of alcohol, bartenders, insurance and cleanup, so compare total quotes.

### Can I hire a bartender without a full service bar?

Yes. Many bartenders work as hybrid service: they bring their tools and staff and use your rented bar and glassware. Confirm their license and insurance.

### Do venues allow dry hire bars?

Some do and some do not. Many Chicago-area venues require licensed, insured bartenders, so ask before you book equipment.

### Who is liable at a dry hire bar?

The host usually is, since they supply the alcohol and the staff. Check with your insurer and the venue, and consider event insurance.

### Do I still need glasses with full service?

Often yes. Some bar companies bring glasses, others use yours. Ask before you rent so you do not pay for duplicates.
