## Choosing the night and the format

Hanukkah is a festival of lights commemorating the rededication of the Temple in Jerusalem. Families light a menorah each night, adding one more candle each evening, and eat foods cooked in oil, such as latkes (potato pancakes) and sufganiyot (jelly doughnuts). Dates change each year on the Gregorian calendar, usually falling in late November or December. Customs vary: some families give gifts every night, some only on one night, some give gelt (coins), and some focus on charity.

For a party, many hosts choose a weekend night or the night that falls closest to a weekend. Consider friends who celebrate other holidays, and decide if your invitation is for a family-and-friends gathering, an office celebration or a community event. For inclusive events, a "winter celebration" can include Hanukkah elements alongside others. ## The menu and the latke plan

Latkes are best hot and crisp, which is hard for a crowd. A common planning figure is about three latkes per guest as part of a meal, and more if latkes are the main event. Fry in batches in the oven or on a griddle, or set up a latke bar with toppings such as applesauce, sour cream, smoked salmon, chives and savory spreads. Keep latkes warm on a sheet pan in a low oven rather than in a covered chafer, which makes them soggy. Frying creates oil and smoke, so plan ventilation, a fire extinguisher and a safe place away from children.

Add brisket, roasted chicken, kugel, salads, and sufganiyot or other sweets. If you keep kosher, confirm with your caterer and use appropriate dishes. If you serve dairy and meat, follow your family's rules. Provide vegetarian and allergy-friendly options too.

For serving, you will need tables, chafing dishes, platters, and plenty of napkins. See [how many chafing dishes for a buffet](/blog/how-many-chafing-dishes-for-a-buffet) for counts, and [buffet line flow](/blog/buffet-line-flow-and-serving-speed) for layouts.

## Candles, safety and the menorah

Place menorahs on a non-flammable surface away from curtains and drafts. Menorah candles are meant to be seen, so many families set them near a window; at a party, a central table works. Provide a shamash (helper candle) for each menorah and a few matches or lighters out of children's reach. If many guests bring menorahs, line them up on a long table, which makes a striking sight. Also keep a small fire extinguisher in reach.

## Games and activities

Dreidel is the classic. Provide a dreidel for every few kids and bowls of gelt or chocolate coins. Add craft tables for painting or candle decorating, a gift swap, or a charity drive. For adults, a trivia game or a song sing-along works well.

## Making it inclusive

Many guests may be unfamiliar with Hanukkah, so explain the lighting briefly and invite everyone to join. Provide the blessings in English and Hebrew on a small card. Welcome guests of other backgrounds, and consider a short story about the holiday. Keep gifts simple if kids of different traditions attend, so no one feels left out. Consider a charity element: a collection for a local food pantry fits the spirit of giving.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do you plan a Hanukkah party?

Pick a night, choose a menu with latkes and sweets, plan seating and a spot for the menorah, and schedule a lighting, a meal and games. Send invitations 3 to 4 weeks ahead and plan for coats in winter.

### How many latkes per person?

A common estimate is about three per guest if latkes are part of a larger meal, and more if they are the main dish. Fry ahead and keep warm in a low oven.

### What do you serve at a Hanukkah party?

Latkes with applesauce and sour cream, brisket or chicken, kugel, salads, and sufganiyot or other desserts. Many hosts add a latke bar with toppings. Offer vegetarian and allergy-friendly options.

### When is Hanukkah?

Hanukkah starts on the 25th of Kislev in the Hebrew calendar, usually in late November or December, and lasts eight nights. Check a current calendar for the year.

### What games are played at Hanukkah?

Dreidel is the classic, played with gelt or chocolate coins. Many families add crafts, gift swaps or trivia. Offer simple instructions for those who have not played.

### Can non-Jewish guests come to a Hanukkah party?

Yes. Many hosts welcome friends and neighbors, and explain the customs briefly. A short welcome and a card with the blessings help everyone participate.

### How do I keep the party safe with candles and frying?

Place menorahs on a non-flammable surface away from curtains, keep a fire extinguisher nearby, and fry latkes in a ventilated area away from children. Never leave lit candles unattended.

### What rentals do I need for a Hanukkah party?

Tables, chairs, linens, serving ware, chafing dishes and, for larger events, a tent or heaters. Add a long table for menorahs and a coat rack in winter.
