Confirmation is a quieter milestone than a wedding or a mitzvah, which is exactly why families get stuck on how big to go. Do you host a backyard open house, a restaurant lunch or a parish hall buffet? The usual pain points are timing, since the ceremony date is set by the parish, and the scale, because relatives and godparents can swell the guest list. This guide helps you decide the size, set a budget and keep the focus on the young person.

## What confirmation is and how families celebrate

In the Catholic Church, confirmation is a sacrament in which a baptized person receives the Holy Spirit and affirms their faith, often in middle school or high school. Many Protestant denominations, including Lutheran, Episcopal and Methodist, also have confirmation or a similar rite. Dates depend on your parish or congregation and on when the bishop or pastor can celebrate the liturgy, so start with the church calendar. Some parishes hold the ceremony on a weekday evening, and others on a weekend, which affects the party choice.

Celebrations range from a small family dinner at home to a full catered lunch in a parish hall. In many families, the sponsor and godparents attend, and gifts often include religious items such as a Bible, a cross or a medal. Customs vary by culture and parish, so ask the person being confirmed what feels right for them.

## Three common formats

**Home open house.** The least expensive and most flexible. Guests come and go over three hours, so you need seating for roughly 70 percent of them. A buffet or catered trays make serving easy, and a tent can handle weather. See [graduation party rentals checklist](/blog/graduation-party-rentals-checklist) for a counting method that works for any open house.

**Restaurant or banquet room.** A private room takes care of food, service and cleanup. Ask about the minimum spend, the room fee and whether you can bring a cake. This suits a seated lunch or dinner of 20 to 60 guests.

**Parish hall.** Many churches offer a hall for members at a modest fee. Ask about kitchen access, tables and chairs, and rules for decorations or alcohol. See [Chicago church hall reception rentals](/blog/chicago-church-hall-reception-rentals).

## What to include

A cake with a simple design, a table for photos and a guest book, a place for cards, a prayer or blessing before the meal, and a short toast by a parent or sponsor are all common. Some families add a slideshow or a keepsake candle. Decor is usually modest: white and gold, red for the Holy Spirit in some traditions, or the teen's favorite color.

## Planning the food and the schedule

A confirmation party works best with a simple schedule. If the ceremony is in the morning or early afternoon, many families head straight to the party, so plan for guests to arrive within an hour of each other and have drinks and a light appetizer ready. A buffet of pasta, chicken, salad and rolls is a safe choice that serves a mixed-age crowd, and a tray of fruit and vegetables covers lighter eaters. If your family has a heritage dish that the teen loves, put it on the table. Cut the cake after the meal, with a short toast from a parent or sponsor, and leave time for photos with each group of relatives. Plan the party to end while energy is still high, rather than letting it fade.

For quantities, a common rule is one main portion per guest plus a small cushion, and two to three side dishes. If you are renting, count a plate, fork, knife, napkin and glass for every guest and a few extra, and use our [plates per guest guide](/blog/how-many-plates-per-guest) to check your numbers. For drinks, water, lemonade and coffee cover most gatherings, and a cooler on the patio keeps the line short.

## Etiquette notes

Invite the sponsor and the immediate family. Confirm with your parish whether there are rules about photographing the ceremony. Thank-you notes follow gifts. If other families are also celebrating, consider a shared reception. If guests are from different faiths, a brief welcome and a blessing set the tone without making anyone uncomfortable. Keep alcohol modest and age appropriate, and follow your own family customs.

## Chicago-area notes

Confirmation seasons in the Chicago area are commonly spring, though dates vary by parish and diocese, so ask your church. Spring weather is unpredictable, with warm sun one day and rain the next, so if you host in a backyard, plan a tent. See [what size tent do I need](/blog/what-size-tent-do-i-need) and the [graduation party tent rental guide](/blog/graduation-party-tent-rental), since confirmations often compete with graduation season for rentals. Parish halls book up quickly in April and May, so reserve early. Check with your village or park district on tent permits.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do you plan a confirmation party?

Confirm the ceremony date, set a guest list and budget, pick a format (home, restaurant or hall), book food and rentals, send invitations, and plan a simple program. Eight to ten weeks is a good lead time.

### How many guests should I invite to a confirmation party?

Many families invite 25 to 75 people, including relatives, the sponsor and a few friends. The right number depends on your budget and space.

### What food should I serve at a confirmation party?

A simple buffet works well: pasta, chicken, salads, bread and a cake. Choose food your family enjoys. Offer vegetarian and allergy-friendly options.

### Do I need a tent for a confirmation party?

If you host outdoors in spring, a tent gives rain cover and shade. Choose a size based on seated guests. See a tent sizing guide and book early.

### What do you give for confirmation?

Common gifts include a cross, a Bible, a medal, a prayer book or a card with a monetary gift. Customs vary by family and culture.

### Who pays for a confirmation party?

Usually the parents, though godparents and relatives may help. Agree on a budget early.

### How long should a confirmation party last?

Two to three hours is typical, with a meal, a toast and cake. An open house format allows guests to come and go.

### Can the party be held at the church?

Many parishes offer a hall for members. Ask about rental fees, kitchen use, tables and chairs and decoration rules.
