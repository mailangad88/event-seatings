**Quick answer**

Build event sponsorship packages in three or four tiers, each with clear benefits, a price and a deadline. Start with the audience you can offer, such as attendees, email lists and local visibility. Price tiers so the top level is a stretch and the entry level is easy to say yes to. Pitch with a short deck, follow up personally, and deliver every benefit you promise.

</div>

Most sponsorship asks fail because the sponsor can't picture what they get. "Support our event" is not an offer. A package that says who will see the logo, where it appears and what the sponsor can do on the day is. Good packages also protect your team from custom requests that eat time, because everyone chooses from the same menu.

Treat these as common practice, not research. Your community, cause and audience will shape what sponsors value. Ask a few friendly sponsors what they would want to see.

## What sponsors actually want

Sponsors usually want one of three things: visibility, access or goodwill. Visibility means logos, signs and mentions to a relevant audience. Access means meeting customers, employees or leaders, through a table, a booth or a speaking slot. Goodwill means being seen as a good community partner, with a story they can tell staff.

Understand which matters most to each prospect. A local bank may want community goodwill and a name on a banner. A software company may want a chance to meet your attendees. Describe your audience honestly, using real numbers such as expected attendance and email list size, and don't overpromise reach.

## Designing the tiers

Start with a top tier, a presenting or title sponsor, who gets the best placement, a speaking moment and a table. Add a mid tier with a table or booth and logo placement, and an entry tier with a logo listing and tickets. Some events add in-kind tiers for food, printing or rentals, and a community or friend level for small donors.

List benefits in a clear grid so sponsors compare easily. Typical benefits include logo placement on signs and the website, tickets or tables, social media mentions, program ads, a booth or display space, a speaking or announcement slot, and naming rights for a part of the event such as the auction or the finish line. Make sure each benefit has a cost and an owner so you can deliver it.

## Pitching and delivering

Create a short package of a one-page overview and a tier grid. Reach out first by a personal note or call, not a mass email. Share how the money is used and give a deadline tied to your print and signage dates. Follow up two or three times, politely.

Delivery matters as much as the sale. Give each sponsor a point of contact, collect logos and approvals early and send a schedule of what will happen when. Give sponsors table space and make their displays look good. This table shows an example structure with illustrative benefits only. Price your own tiers based on your audience and costs, and test them with a few friendly sponsors.

|---|---|---|
| Presenting | Naming, top logo, speaking slot, table, tickets | A major local business or partner |
| Gold | Logo on signs, table, tickets, social mention | Established local companies |

## Steps to build and sell packages

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Know your audience.</strong> Gather expected attendance, demographics and reach.</li>
<li><strong>Design three or four tiers.</strong> Make the difference between them clear.</li>
<li><strong>Price with a rationale.</strong> Tie prices to costs and value, not guesses.</li>
<li><strong>Create a one-page pitch.</strong> Include benefits, deadline and contact.</li>
<li><strong>Reach out personally.</strong> Start with warm contacts and board connections.</li>
<li><strong>Deliver and report.</strong> Fulfill benefits and send a recap with photos.</li>
</ol>

## Chicago-area notes

Chicago sponsors range from neighborhood businesses to large employers, so pitch locally. Chambers of commerce, business associations and park district partners are common starting points for community events. If your event needs permits or signage at a park or street, ask the permitting authority about rules for sponsor banners and naming, since many public sites restrict commercial signage. Be careful about alcohol or other regulated sponsors, and check with the venue, village or park district. Winter and summer events draw different sponsors, so match the pitch to the season.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are event sponsorship packages?

They are tiered offers that list what a sponsor receives, such as logo placement, tickets, a table or a speaking slot, in exchange for a set amount of support.

### How many sponsorship tiers should I offer?

Three or four is enough for most events. Too many tiers confuse sponsors and add work.

### How do I price sponsorship packages?

Tie prices to your costs and the value of the audience, and test them with a few friendly prospects. Ask similar local events what they offer, without guessing at their prices.

### When should I start asking for sponsors?

Many organizers start four to six months before the event, so sponsors can plan budgets and you can print signage.

### What should be in a sponsorship pitch?

A short overview of the event and cause, the audience, a tier grid with benefits and prices, a deadline and a contact.

### What is an in-kind sponsorship?

It is a donation of goods or services, such as printing, food or rentals, in exchange for recognition. Agree on the value and credit in writing.

### How do I keep sponsors happy?

Deliver every promised benefit, give them a point of contact, send a recap and thank them promptly.
