**Quick answer**

Twelve months gives you room to breathe. Spend months 12 to 10 on the budget, guest list and venue, months 9 to 7 on the big vendors and attire, months 6 to 4 on invitations, rentals and details, and the last three months on RSVPs, seating and the day-of schedule. Book the venue first, since it fixes your date and guest limit.

</div>

Most couples do not struggle with any single task. They struggle with order. You book a caterer before you know the venue allows outside food, or you send invitations before the guest list is final, and suddenly you are redoing work. A timeline puts the decisions in the right sequence so each one makes the next one easier.

## Rules of thumb for a 12-month plan

These are common planning benchmarks, not rules. Your venue and your calendar will move them around.

## Months 12 to 10: the foundation

Start with three conversations, in this order: who is paying for what, how many people you honestly want to invite, and what kind of day you picture. Those answers set the budget and a rough guest count, and every other choice hangs on them. Our guide to [how to set a wedding budget](/blog/how-to-set-a-wedding-budget) walks through a simple split, and [how much a wedding costs in Chicago](/blog/how-much-does-a-wedding-cost-in-chicago) gives context for the numbers.

Next, tour venues and book one. The venue decides your date, your capacity, your catering rules and whether you need rentals at all. Read [how to choose a wedding venue in Chicago](/blog/how-to-choose-a-wedding-venue-chicago) before you start touring, and bring a list of questions. Once the date is locked, you can sensibly think about everything else.

## Months 9 to 7: the big vendors

This is when the vendors who can only work one wedding per day get claimed. Photographers, bands or DJs, caterers and officiants are the usual first wave. If you want a planner or day-of coordinator, hire one now, because they can help you avoid expensive early mistakes. See [wedding planner vs day-of coordinator](/blog/wedding-planner-vs-day-of-coordinator) to decide which fits, and [the wedding vendor booking order](/blog/wedding-vendor-booking-order) for which calls to make first.

This is also a good window to shop for attire, since dresses and suits may take months to arrive and be altered. Reserve a hotel block for out-of-town guests, as covered in [hotel room blocks for wedding guests](/blog/hotel-room-blocks-for-wedding-guests).

## Months 6 to 4: details and invitations

Now the day gets specific. Decide on the ceremony format, the reception layout, the menu and the look of the room. This is the right time to sketch your rentals: chairs, tables, linens, tents and dance floor. Start with [the complete wedding rental checklist](/blog/complete-wedding-rental-checklist) and [wedding rentals by guest count](/blog/wedding-reception-rentals-by-guest-count), then request a quote so your decor budget is based on real prices.

Send save-the-dates around six to eight months out if you have destination or holiday-weekend guests, and design invitations. The timing details are in [when to send save the dates and invitations](/blog/when-to-send-save-the-dates-and-invitations).

## Months 3 to 1: the closing stretch

Formal invitations go out roughly two months before the day. Set an RSVP deadline three to four weeks before the wedding so you have time to chase stragglers (see [wedding RSVP deadlines and follow up](/blog/wedding-rsvp-deadline-and-follow-up)). Once responses are in, build the seating chart with our [wedding seating chart guide](/blog/wedding-seating-chart-how-to), confirm your final count with the caterer, and write the schedule using the [wedding day timeline template](/blog/wedding-day-timeline-template).

## What to do each quarter

Use this as a checklist. If you have fewer than twelve months, compress the first column and decide faster.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Window | Main tasks | Decisions it unlocks |
|---|---|---|
| 12 to 10 months | Budget, rough guest count, tour and book venue | Date, capacity, catering rules |
| 9 to 7 months | Photographer, music, caterer, officiant, attire | Style, schedule, menu |
| 6 to 4 months | Rental plan, decor, invitations designed, hotel block | Quote for rentals, mailing list |
| 3 to 2 months | Invitations out, tastings, license, vows | RSVP count, final menu |
| 1 month to day of | RSVPs chased, seating chart, schedule, final payments | Final headcount, rentals locked |

</div>

Rental orders are usually finalized in the last month, but the decisions behind them (round or long tables, ceremony flip or second set of chairs) are easier to make earlier.

## How to use this timeline

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Set the date range.</strong> Pick two or three possible weekends before you tour venues.</li>
<li><strong>Fix the budget and the guest ceiling.</strong> Everything else scales from these two numbers.</li>
<li><strong>Book the venue, then the vendors who sell out first.</strong> Photo, music, catering and officiant.</li>
<li><strong>Plan rentals around your venue's inclusions.</strong> Ask what tables, chairs and linens are already provided.</li>
<li><strong>Send invitations, then chase RSVPs.</strong> Use a simple tracker and set one firm deadline.</li>
<li><strong>Write the day-of schedule and share it a week early.</strong> Every vendor should see the same one-page plan.</li>
</ol>

## Chicago-area notes

Chicago has a real wedding calendar. Late spring through early fall books up first, and Saturdays in June, September and early October are in high demand, so lean toward the earlier side of the timeline for those dates. Winter and early spring weddings are often easier to book and can cost less, but plan for coats, slush and snow removal at outdoor entrances. If you want an outdoor ceremony, line up an indoor backup when you book the venue, since lake-influenced weather can turn quickly. For tents or public park sites, check with your village or park district about permits well ahead of time. Our [Chicago tent permit guide](/blog/chicago-tent-permit) is a good starting point.

## Frequently asked questions

### How far in advance should you start planning a wedding?

Many couples start twelve months out, which gives enough time to book a venue and key vendors for a popular date. If you want a Saturday in peak season, start earlier. If you have less time, a shorter plan works too, as covered in our guide to [planning a wedding in six months](/blog/how-to-plan-a-wedding-in-6-months).

### What should I book first when planning a wedding?

Book the venue and date first, because nearly every other choice depends on them. After that, secure the vendors who take one wedding per day, such as the photographer, band or DJ, and caterer if your venue does not provide one.

### When should I order wedding rentals?

Many planners finalize the rental order four to six weeks before the wedding, but request a quote earlier so you know the cost. For peak dates, reserving tents and specialty items sooner is wise. Ask for the delivery and pickup windows too.

### Can I plan a wedding in 12 months while working full time?

Yes. A twelve-month timeline is designed to spread the work into a few hours a week. Batch your tasks, such as venue tours on one weekend and tastings on another, and delegate research to someone you trust.

### When should I send save the dates?

Six to eight months before the wedding is common, and earlier for holiday weekends or destination-style weddings with travel. Formal invitations then follow about eight to ten weeks before the date.

### What is the most common timeline mistake?

Sending invitations before the guest list and venue capacity are settled. If you have to remove or add people afterward, you create awkward conversations and extra printing costs. Lock the list first.

### Do I need a wedding planner to follow a timeline?

No, but a planner or day-of coordinator can keep the plan moving and catch problems early. Many couples use a timeline and a tracker and hire a coordinator only for the last month.
