**Quick answer**

A certificate of insurance (COI) is a one-page document from an insurer that proves a vendor carries liability coverage and, often, names your venue or company as an additional insured. Venues and corporate hosts commonly ask for one from every vendor, including rental companies. Ask your venue for its written requirements two to four weeks before the event, then give them to each vendor in time.

</div>

A COI is the paperwork that shows up at the worst time: three days before your event, the building manager emails that the rental company needs one on file or the delivery won't be allowed. It is not complicated, but it takes a few days to get from an insurer, and rules differ by venue. This guide is general information, not legal or insurance advice. Always check the specific requirements of your venue and your own insurer.

## What a COI is

A certificate of insurance summarizes coverage: the insurer, the policy number, the dates and the limits. An **additional insured** endorsement means the vendor's policy also covers another party, such as your venue or company, for claims that arise from the vendor's work. A **certificate holder** is the party the COI is addressed to. Venues often require both, and they list the exact wording they want.

## What venues and buildings commonly ask for

Requirements vary, so there is no single list. Typical items you may see include:

| Request | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| General liability | Coverage for injury or property damage caused by the vendor |
| Additional insured | The venue, landlord or host named on the vendor's policy |
| Workers' compensation | Coverage for the vendor's employees on site |
| Auto liability | Coverage for delivery vehicles |
| Waiver of subrogation | The insurer gives up the right to sue the venue |
| Minimum limits | A dollar amount the venue sets |

We do not state specific limits, because they depend on the venue. Ask for the written requirement instead of guessing. Some office towers publish an insurance form for contractors, and some park districts and forest preserves publish theirs for permit holders. Ask the manager for the document.

## Who needs what

- **Host or sponsor:** sometimes needs event liability coverage. A corporation may have its own policy that covers events; ask your risk manager.
- **Rental company:** often provides a COI for its delivery and setup work.
- **Caterer, bartender and DJ:** each may need to provide a COI, and alcohol service may require liquor liability.
- **Venue:** may require all vendors to be on an approved list.

## Steps to stay out of trouble

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Ask the venue</strong> for its insurance and vendor rules in writing.</li>
<li><strong>List the vendors</strong> and give each the exact wording.</li>
<li><strong>Collect COIs</strong> at least a week before the event.</li>
<li><strong>Check</strong> dates, names and wording against the venue's form.</li>
<li><strong>Send</strong> to the venue and keep copies.</li>
</ol>

## Common mistakes

- Waiting until delivery week to ask for a COI
- Using a certificate holder name that does not match the legal name of the venue
- Forgetting a vendor, such as the DJ or the photographer
- Letting a certificate expire before the event date
- Assuming that the venue's own insurance covers everyone

## Other vendor requirements

Some buildings ask vendors to follow load-in schedules, use freight elevators, wear badges, protect floors and haul away their own trash. Hotels and convention centers may require union labor for some work, so ask. Public venues might require a permit. Do not assume that a requirement is the same across venues; check each. For sites such as a park, see our post on [Chicago tent permits](/blog/chicago-tent-permit) and ask the park district about its insurance requirements.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a certificate of insurance?

A one-page document from an insurer that shows a vendor's coverage, policy dates and limits. Venues use it to confirm vendors are insured.

### What does additional insured mean?

It adds another party, such as the venue, to the vendor's policy for claims arising from the vendor's work. Venues usually give the exact wording.

### How long does it take to get a COI?

Often a few business days, but it varies. Ask your venue early and ask vendors to request it right away.

### Do rental companies need to provide a COI?

Many venues require it. Ask your venue and tell the rental company during planning.

### Does the host need insurance?

Sometimes. Corporate hosts may be covered by an existing policy; ask your risk manager. Some venues require event liability coverage.

### What if the vendor cannot meet the venue's limits?

Tell the venue early. They may accept alternatives or you may need another vendor. Do not wait until the day of the event.
