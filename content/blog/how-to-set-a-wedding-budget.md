**Quick answer**

Start with the total you can truly spend, including contributions from family, then hold back a cushion of about 10 to 15 percent. Split the rest by category, fixing venue and catering first because they take the largest share. Rank your priorities so the money follows what matters most, and track actual costs against estimates in one spreadsheet from the first deposit on.

</div>

A budget fails when it is a wish instead of a decision. Many couples write down a total, then discover the venue alone eats most of it. Setting a wedding budget is really about three choices: how much money exists, what matters most, and what you are willing to give up. Once those are written down, the rest is arithmetic.

## Step one: find the real total

Add up every source: your own savings, monthly contributions between now and the wedding, and any family gifts. Have the family conversation early and kindly. Ask what each person wants to contribute, whether the money comes with expectations, and whether it is a gift or a promise with a date. Write down the total and a date by which each contribution will arrive.

Then subtract the cushion. Weddings almost always cost more than the early estimate because of taxes, service fees, alterations and last-minute additions. Holding back 10 to 15 percent keeps a surprise from becoming debt.

## Step two: choose priorities before categories

Before you divide anything, each of you should name the three things that would make the day feel right. It might be live music, an open bar, a photographer you love, or a room full of family. Compare lists and settle the overlap. Priorities let you spend generously where it matters and plainly where it does not.

For a sample category split, see [how much a wedding costs in Chicago](/blog/how-much-does-a-wedding-cost-in-chicago) and our [wedding rental budget breakdown](/blog/wedding-rental-budget-breakdown). Both show common shares of the total, which you can adjust.

## Step three: lock the big items, then trim

Venue and catering will set most of your numbers, so decide them first. Get all-in quotes, compare them per guest, and see what your budget allows. If you are over, change the guest count, date or day of the week before cutting quality. A shorter guest list is the cleanest way to save, as shown in [how to cut a wedding guest list](/blog/how-to-cut-a-wedding-guest-list).

Then allocate the remaining categories and keep a line called Miscellaneous. When one category goes over, take the difference from another category, not from the cushion.

## A sample allocation worksheet

Fill in the right column with your own total. The percentages below are a common starting point, not a rule.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Category | Starting share | Move up if... |
|---|---|---|
| Venue and catering | 45 to 50% | You want a premium room or open bar |
| Photo and video | 10 to 12% | Photos matter most to you |
| Music and entertainment | 8 to 10% | You want a live band |
| Flowers and decor | 8 to 10% | You want a lush ceremony |
| Rentals | 6 to 8% | Your venue is an empty shell |
| Attire and beauty | 6 to 8% | You are buying custom outfits |
| Other (stationery, license, tips) | 5 to 8% | You have many out-of-town guests |

</div>

The shares add up to more than 100 percent at the high ends, which is the point: not everything can be high. Choose where you go high and where you go low.

## How to set the budget in one sitting

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Write the total and the sources.</strong> Include dates when money will arrive.</li>
<li><strong>Subtract the cushion.</strong> That is your working budget.</li>
<li><strong>Name your top three priorities.</strong> Each person picks, then you compare.</li>
<li><strong>Get quotes for venue and catering.</strong> Compare per guest and all-in.</li>
<li><strong>Allocate the rest and track.</strong> Use a spreadsheet you both can edit.</li>
</ol>

## Keeping the budget honest after you set it

A budget only works if you look at it. Pick a regular time each week, even ten minutes, to log new quotes, deposits and payments. Mark each line as estimate, quoted or paid so you can tell how much of the total is real. When you see a category drifting, deal with it right away instead of waiting for the final invoice.

Share the sheet with the people who are contributing, if they want to see it. Transparency reduces the awkward conversations later. Many couples also agree on a rule that any purchase over a set amount gets a quick discussion first, which stops small impulse upgrades from adding up to a large number.

## Chicago-area notes

In the Chicago area, ask every venue for the full cost, including parking, valet, freight elevator rules, security, insurance certificates and coat check. Downtown venues may have these extras more often than suburban halls. If you plan a backyard or park wedding, remember rentals, portable restrooms, permits and possible tent costs. For park sites, check with your village or park district for current rules. Winter weddings may reduce venue fees but add heat, coat racks and weather contingency.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I decide how much to spend on a wedding?

Start with what you can pay without debt: savings, monthly contributions and confirmed family gifts. Subtract a cushion of 10 to 15 percent for surprises. The remainder is your working budget.

### What percentage of my budget should go to the venue?

Many couples spend around 45 to 50 percent on venue and catering combined. If your venue includes tables, chairs and decor, the share may be higher but your rental line smaller.

### Who pays for what at a wedding?

Traditions vary, and many couples now split costs among themselves and both families. The best approach is to talk openly early and put every commitment in writing, including amounts and dates.

### How do I track wedding expenses?

Use one spreadsheet with columns for estimate, quote, deposit, balance and due date. Update it every time you sign or pay something. Our [Event Budget Spreadsheet](/downloads/event-budget-spreadsheet) is built for this.

### What should I do if I go over budget?

Take the overage from another category, not from your cushion. Review the biggest levers first: guest count, date and venue inclusions. Small decor cuts rarely close a big gap.

### Should I include a cushion in my wedding budget?

Yes. Many planners hold back 10 to 15 percent because taxes, fees, alterations and last-minute additions are common. If you do not use it, it becomes savings.

### Is it better to have fewer guests or a smaller venue?

Fewer guests lowers nearly every cost at once, including food, rentals and invitations. A smaller venue helps only if it also lowers the minimum spend.
