Seating charts feel high stakes because every guest notices where they land. The good news is that most of the work is logic, not diplomacy. When you know your table sizes, your anchor groups and your two or three hard cases, the rest falls into place. Here is a process that works for weddings, banquets, fundraisers, birthdays and company dinners.

## Step 1: Choose table sizes and count

Divide your confirmed guests by the seats per table. A 60-inch round fits eight comfortably, and a 72-inch round fits ten. Long banquet tables suit family style and tight rooms. See [how many tables for 50, 100, 150 or 200 guests](/blog/how-many-tables-for-50-100-150-200-guests) and [long vs round tables](/blog/long-tables-vs-round-tables-wedding). A few seats short of full is kinder to guests than packing every chair.

## Step 2: Place the host table

Put the host, guest of honor or head table where everyone can see it and away from loud speakers. Some hosts prefer a sweetheart table or a table mixed in with guests. Our post on [head table versus sweetheart table layouts](/blog/head-table-vs-sweetheart-table-layout) compares them.

## Step 3: Anchor the groups

List the groups that must sit together, such as parents and siblings, a college friend group or a department. Give each a table or two. Fill the group first, then add one or two outside guests per table so conversation mixes.

## Step 4: Mix in the singles

People who arrive alone do best at tables with others who have something in common, like an age range, a hobby or a shared friend. Avoid isolating them at a table full of couples.

## Step 5: Handle the hard cases

Every list has two or three. Families that are not on speaking terms, or exes, can be seated apart but in view of the host. An older relative may want to be near the restrooms and far from the speakers. Parents of the host often sit near the front. Guests with mobility needs should have an easy route and extra space. See [ADA accessible seating and pathways](/blog/ada-accessible-event-seating-and-pathways).

## Step 6: Check the room

Walk the layout in your head or on paper. Are aisles wide enough for servers? Is the dance floor, bar and buffet reachable without crossing a speaker's path? Our [reception layout mistakes to avoid](/blog/reception-layout-mistakes-to-avoid) is a quick reality check.

## Step 7: Display it

Use a printed chart, a sign, escort cards or a mirror. See [mirror seating chart display](/blog/mirror-seating-chart-display-guide) and [reception table names and seating maps](/blog/reception-table-names-and-seating-map). Give a copy to your venue or caterer so staff can help guests find their seats, and keep a few blank cards for last-minute additions.

## Do you even need assigned seating?

For casual parties, open seating is fine, especially at open houses where people come and go. For plated meals, weddings and formal dinners, assigned seating keeps service organized and prevents guests from wandering. A middle path is assigned tables with open seats.

## Chicago-area notes

Many Chicago-area banquet halls and village spaces have fixed table counts and set-up rules, so ask what the room can hold before you design. Winter events also need space for coats, and a crowded entry makes a seating display hard to read. Outdoor events on lawns need level ground for table legs and room for tent poles. Check with your venue or your village or park district for room limits, and ask about fire code aisle widths. A rental company can help you test layouts for chairs and tables.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I make a seating chart?

Get your final RSVPs, choose table sizes, and seat the host table first. Then place family and friend groups and fill with singles and tricky cases. Print a map and table cards.

### How many people should sit at each table?

Eight to ten is the most common for round tables. Smaller tables of six work for intimate dinners, and long tables suit family style. Match the table size to your room and your guest count.

### How do I seat divorced parents at an event?

Seat them at separate tables near the host, with each at a table of supportive family. Both should have a prominent spot. Speak to each in advance so there are no surprises.

### Where should the head table go?

Put it where it is visible to all guests, often at the front of the room and away from doors and the kitchen. It should not be blocked by the DJ or buffet. Make sure the route to the dance floor is clear.

### Do I need a seating chart for a birthday party?

Not always. Casual parties do well with open seating. If you have a sit-down dinner with more than about 20 guests, assigned tables help service and make guests feel looked after.

### How do I seat guests who do not know anyone?

Place them with people who share a likely interest or who are known to be welcoming. Introduce them to a table-mate before dinner. Avoid seating them by themselves at the edge.

### What if guests change seats?

Let them. A chart is a plan for serving and for first impressions, not a rule. If meals are plated, make sure someone tells the caterer which table counts changed.
