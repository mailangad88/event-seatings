**Quick answer**

Start with a total number you can truly spend, then divide it into categories before you book anything. A common starting split is about 40 to 50 percent for food and drink, 15 to 25 percent for the venue, 10 to 15 percent for rentals, and the rest for entertainment, photos, decor and a cushion. Adjust to your priorities, then track actual costs next to estimates.

</div>

Budgets go wrong when they are built from quotes instead of limits. You collect three prices, love the best one and discover later that the total is far above what you can pay. A better method is to set a ceiling first, split it by category, then shop inside each slice. This guide shows how to do that for any event, with a table you can adapt and a plan for the costs people forget.

## Step 1: Set a ceiling you can really pay

Decide the maximum total, including who is contributing. Many hosts also set a comfortable number and a hard limit, so they know where the stretch begins. Write both down.

## Step 2: Choose your top two priorities

Every event has things that matter more. Maybe great food and a good photographer, or a bigger guest list and a simpler menu. Pick two priorities and let them get more of the budget. Everything else gets less. This one choice does more than any spreadsheet trick.

## Step 3: Split the ceiling by category

Use the percentages above as a draft, then adjust. A sit-down dinner raises the food share, while a cocktail party lowers it and raises bar costs. An event at home removes venue costs, but usually adds rentals and cleanup. See [the cost of a backyard party versus a venue](/blog/backyard-party-vs-venue-cost) for that tradeoff.

## Step 4: List the costs people forget

These are the line items that break budgets:

- Delivery, setup and pickup fees. Our guide to [rental delivery fees](/blog/event-rental-delivery-fees-explained) explains them.
- Service charges, gratuities and taxes, which may be added to caterer quotes.
- Damage waivers, breakage and deposits. See [rental deposits explained](/blog/rental-deposits-explained).
- Extra ice, trash bags, paper goods and parking.
- Event insurance or a certificate of insurance for some venues.
- Tips for staff and drivers.

## Step 5: Get at least three quotes per big category

Compare quotes line by line, not by total. [How to compare event rental quotes](/blog/how-to-compare-event-rental-quotes) shows what to check. Ask what is included in each price, and what the cancellation terms are.

## Step 6: Track estimates and actuals

Make three columns: estimate, quote and paid. Update them whenever a number changes. Check the sheet every week, and look at the cushion line first. If it shrinks early, trim a lower priority item right away rather than at the end.

## Ways to save without hurting the event

Many planners trim cost by shortening the event by an hour, choosing a weekday or off-peak date, narrowing the menu, and combining rentals in one order. For specific ideas, read [how to save money on event rentals](/blog/how-to-save-money-on-event-rentals) and [the event rental budget by guest count](/blog/event-rental-budget-by-guest-count).

## Chicago-area notes

Rough Chicago-area costs vary widely by neighborhood, suburb, season and day of week, so treat any range you read online as a starting point and get quotes. Saturday evenings in summer and fall, and December weekends, usually cost more than weekday or off-season dates. Downtown venues may add loading, freight elevator or parking charges, and some building rules require insurance certificates. See [Chicago high-rise freight elevator and COI](/blog/chicago-high-rise-party-freight-elevator-coi). For permits in parks or on streets, check with your village or park district, since fees differ.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I make an event budget?

Set a total you can spend, name your top priorities, and split the total into categories like food, venue, rentals and entertainment. Then collect quotes inside each slice. Track estimates against actual costs as you go.

### What percentage of the budget should go to food?

Food and drink often takes the largest share, commonly 40 to 50 percent for a catered event. A cocktail party or a casual backyard meal can come in lower. Your priorities should guide the final number.

### How much cushion should I add to an event budget?

Many planners hold back around ten to fifteen percent for surprises such as fees, count changes and weather plans. Smaller events can use less. Treat the cushion as spendable only after every main vendor is booked.

### How do I stay on budget when planning an event?

Track every cost in one sheet and compare it to the plan weekly. Decide up front which items you will cut first. Avoid changing the headcount late, since that moves food and rental costs.

### What are hidden event costs?

Delivery fees, service charges, taxes, gratuities, damage waivers, extra staff and insurance are common. Ask each vendor for an all-in estimate. Read contracts for overtime and late fees.

### How do I budget for a party at home?

Skip the venue line, then raise rentals, food and cleanup. Count tables, chairs, linens and plates for your guest number. Add a small allowance for ice, paper goods and a weather plan.

### Should I pay for the event with a deposit or in full?

Most vendors ask for a deposit to hold the date and the balance close to the event. Pay in a way that keeps a written record. Read the cancellation terms before you pay.
