A bar mitzvah has two jobs: a meaningful religious milestone for your son and a celebration that gathers family and friends. The planning pain is that these run on different clocks. The synagogue sets the service date and the study schedule, while venues and caterers book on the party calendar. This timeline keeps both in step and leaves room for your community's own customs.

## Start with the synagogue, not the venue

The bar mitzvah date is usually tied to your son's Hebrew birthday and the weekly Torah portion, and each synagogue has its own scheduling. Some communities hold the service on Shabbat morning, some on Monday or Thursday when the Torah is read, and some offer a different date entirely. Ask the clergy early how dates are assigned, when the study program begins, and any rules about Shabbat observance, which can affect whether the party is on Saturday night, Sunday or another day.

If your community is Orthodox or traditional, there may be rules about music, food (kosher catering) and timing on Shabbat. In Reform and many Conservative communities, families often hold the celebration Saturday night or Sunday. Once you have the service date, the party date follows, and you can start touring venues. For seating specifics, see our guide to [bar and bat mitzvah seating](/blog/bar-and-bat-mitzvah-seating).

## Month by month checklist

**18 to 12 months out.** Talk with the synagogue about the date, the study program and any fees. Set a budget and a guest range. Tour venues and ask about kosher or kosher-style catering if that matters to you. Start a guest list by family branch, friends and classmates.

**12 to 9 months out.** Sign with the venue, the caterer and the DJ or band. Choose a photographer. Decide whether you want a candle lighting, a hora with chairs, a video montage or a mitzvah project display, and which are appropriate for your community.

**8 to 4 months out.** Work with the tutor or cantor on preparation. Order the tallit and kippot, which many families do through the synagogue or a Judaica shop. Plan the theme or color scheme if you want one. Book rentals and decor.

**3 months out.** Send save-the-dates and, around 8 to 10 weeks out, invitations. Include the service time, the reception details, and any dress guidance for the synagogue.

**1 month out.** Collect RSVPs, build the seating chart, confirm the run of show, and arrange transportation for out-of-town family.

**Event week.** Review the timeline with the DJ, confirm rental delivery, prepare thank-you notes, and rest.

## What to do in the final month

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Finalize the guest list.</strong> Chase every RSVP and note dietary needs, including kosher or allergy requirements.</li>
<li><strong>Build the seating chart.</strong> Seat family by branch, put friends near the dance floor, and give older relatives easy access to the restrooms.</li>
<li><strong>Write the run of show.</strong> Include the blessing over the challah, the meal, speeches, the candle lighting if used, and dance sets.</li>
<li><strong>Brief the vendors.</strong> Send the caterer, DJ and photographer the same document.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm rentals and delivery.</strong> Check tables, chairs, linens and the dance floor.</li>
<li><strong>Prepare the family.</strong> Decide who gives toasts and who handles the gifts and cards.</li>
</ol>

## Tips that save stress

Ask your synagogue what a reasonable seating arrangement for the service looks like, because the number of guests who attend services and the number who attend the party can differ. Build a short grace period into the reception schedule, since a service that runs long should not push back dinner. If your guest list includes both observant and non-observant relatives, communicate clearly about dress, phones and food. Set a plan for kids: many families host a separate kids' table or activities. Keep the speeches short and in order. Finally, assign someone not in the family to hold the gift envelopes and card box so nothing disappears. For decor ideas that match the milestone, see our [bat mitzvah party planning ideas](/blog/bat-mitzvah-party-planning-ideas), many of which apply to a bar mitzvah too.

## Chicago-area notes

The Chicago area has a large and varied Jewish community, with synagogues and event spaces across the city and the north and northwest suburbs. Because spring and fall are popular for mitzvahs, venues and caterers with kosher experience book early, so start with the synagogue date and move fast. In winter, plan for coats and parking, and check weather contingencies for any outdoor photos. If you hold the party at a synagogue hall, ask what furniture is on site before you rent. Catering rules and certification vary, so confirm with your rabbi what level of kashrut your community expects. For tent or outdoor receptions, check with your village or park district on permits.

## Frequently asked questions

### When should you start planning a bar mitzvah?

Most families start 12 to 18 months ahead. The first step is to confirm the date with your synagogue, then book the venue and caterer. Popular spring and fall weekends fill up fast.

### Who pays for a bar mitzvah?

Traditionally the parents pay, and some families receive help from grandparents or relatives. There is no single rule, so decide as a family what you can afford and set a total budget early.

### How many guests are typical for a bar mitzvah?

Guest counts range from a few dozen at a small family party to 200 or more. The count depends on your community, family size and budget. Choose a number before you tour venues.

### How long does a bar mitzvah celebration last?

A reception commonly runs three to five hours, with cocktails, dinner, speeches and dancing. If the service is in the morning, the party may be a lunch or a separate evening event.

### When do you send bar mitzvah invitations?

Many families mail invitations eight to ten weeks ahead and ask for RSVPs about three to four weeks before the party. Send save-the-dates earlier if guests will travel.

### What is the difference between a bar mitzvah and a bat mitzvah?

Both mark a young person taking on Jewish religious responsibility, traditionally at age 13 for a boy (bar) and 12 or 13 for a girl (bat), though practice varies by community. The planning timeline is very similar.

### Does a bar mitzvah have to be on Saturday?

No. Many are on Shabbat, but some are on Monday or Thursday mornings, or on another date depending on the synagogue. Parties are often Saturday night or Sunday. Ask your clergy.

### What rentals do I need for a bar mitzvah reception?

Typically round guest tables, chairs, linens, a head or family table, a dance floor, cake and gift tables, and sometimes a candle lighting table. Ask the venue what is included before you order.
