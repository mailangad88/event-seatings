**Quick answer**

To measure event ROI, set a clear goal before the event, track every cost, and measure the outcome that matches the goal, such as revenue, leads, donations or retention. Then use a simple formula: net benefit divided by total cost. When the benefit isn't money, compare cost per attendee, per lead or per outcome with past events. Survey attendees and follow up within two weeks.

Leadership asks, "Was it worth it?" and the planner has a folder of photos and a feeling. That is not enough. Measuring return on an event doesn't require complex analytics, just a plan made before the event: what you wanted, what you spent and how you will count the result. Even a rough, honest calculation puts you ahead of most.

There is no universal benchmark for what a good return looks like. It depends on your goal, industry and audience. The most useful comparison is your own past events.

## Start with a goal that can be measured

Different events have different goals, and the metric must match. A sales event looks at pipeline created or deals influenced. A fundraiser looks at net dollars raised and new donors. An employee event may track attendance, survey scores and retention over time. A conference may look at registrations, sponsor renewals and satisfaction.

Write the goal as a number and a date: "Raise $60,000 net," "Generate 40 qualified leads within 30 days," or "Reach 80 percent of staff and a satisfaction score of 4 out of 5." If you can't attach a number, write a clear, observable outcome. Then decide how you will collect the data, before the event.

## Count all the costs

Costs include the obvious ones such as venue, food, rentals, AV, printing, travel and entertainment, plus the less obvious: staff time, marketing, software, insurance, permits and taxes. Staff time is often the largest hidden cost, so estimate hours and a loaded hourly rate if your organization tracks them. Use a budget sheet such as the [Event Budget Spreadsheet](/downloads/event-budget-spreadsheet) with both estimated and actual columns. Our [corporate event budget per person](/blog/corporate-event-budget-per-person) guide shows the usual categories.

Record the final actuals once invoices are in, and note any in-kind donations at fair value so you can see the full picture.

## Do the math and tell the story

The basic formula is: ROI = (benefit minus cost) divided by cost. If an event raised $50,000 net of ticket costs and the total cost was $20,000, the return is $30,000 divided by $20,000, or 1.5, which is 150 percent. For non-monetary goals, use ratios such as cost per attendee, cost per lead or cost per new donor, and compare across events.

Add context. Attach survey results, quotes, photos and notes on what to change. Present a one-page summary: goal, cost, result, lessons and a recommendation. Leaders respond to a short, honest summary more than a long deck. See [how to increase event attendance](/blog/how-to-increase-event-attendance) for ideas on improving the numbers next time.

## Common measurement mistakes

The most common mistake is deciding what to measure after the event, when the data is gone. Close behind is ignoring staff time, which makes events look cheaper than they are. Others include counting registrations instead of attendance, treating every lead as equal, and surveying so late that people forget the details. A short, consistent process beats a clever one that nobody has time to repeat. Keep the same questions each year, store the results in one place and review them before you start planning the next event.

## Metrics by event type

This table suggests metrics that match common event goals. Choose one to three, not all.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Event type | Primary metric | Supporting metrics |
|---|---|---|
| Sales or client event | Pipeline or deals influenced | Attendance, meetings booked |
| Fundraiser or gala | Net dollars raised | New donors, sponsor renewals |
| Conference | Registrations and revenue | Sponsor satisfaction, survey score |
| Employee event | Attendance rate | Survey score, feedback themes |
| Community event | Attendance and sign-ups | Volunteers recruited, press or social reach |

## Steps to measure ROI

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Set the goal.</strong> Define a number, a date and a method of measurement.</li>
<li><strong>Build the budget.</strong> Include staff time and in-kind items.</li>
<li><strong>Collect data during the event.</strong> Check-in counts, leads, donations and photos.</li>
<li><strong>Survey attendees.</strong> Short, within 48 hours.</li>
<li><strong>Follow up.</strong> Track leads, gifts or outcomes for two to four weeks.</li>
<li><strong>Report and learn.</strong> One-page summary with lessons and next steps.</li>
</ol>

## Chicago-area notes

In Chicago, cost drivers like venue, parking, labor and weather can change from season to season, so compare events from the same time of year when you can. A downtown December event may cost more than a summer park event, even with a similar guest count. Track weather and competing events, since they affect attendance. If your event used a park or street, note permit and insurance costs. For comparing rentals, request an itemized quote and keep it with your records. If you are unsure about local fees, check with your village or park district and record the actual amount paid.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do you measure the ROI of an event?

Set a goal, track all costs, measure the outcome that matches the goal and compare. A simple formula is net benefit divided by total cost.

### What is a good ROI for an event?

There is no universal number. It depends on your goal and audience. Compare your results with your own past events and with your stated targets.

### How do I measure ROI if the goal isn't revenue?

Use cost per attendee, cost per lead or cost per outcome, plus survey results. Compare across events.

### What costs should I include?

Venue, food, rentals, AV, printing, travel, entertainment, marketing, software, insurance, permits and staff time.

### When should I send an event survey?

Within 48 hours, while the experience is fresh. Keep it short and focus on a few key questions.

### How long should I track results after an event?

Two to four weeks is common for leads or donations, and longer for sales cycles. Set the window in advance.

### What should be in an event report?

The goal, total cost, results, survey highlights, lessons and recommendations on one page.
