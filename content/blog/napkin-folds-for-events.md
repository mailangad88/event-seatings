**Quick answer**

For most events, pick one of three folds: the flat rectangle (fastest, formal), the pocket fold (holds a menu or flatware) or the pleated fan (best in a water glass or under a napkin ring). A 20 x 20-inch cloth napkin handles all of them. Fancy shapes like the bishop's hat look great but take about a minute each and collapse easily, so skip them for 100 or more guests unless someone is folding in advance.

</div>

A cloth napkin is the cheapest way to make a place setting look planned. The trouble in Chicago-area ballrooms and tents is volume: at 150 guests, a fold that takes 20 seconds is 50 minutes of work, and a fold that takes a minute is two and a half hours. This guide ranks seven folds by look, speed and how well they survive a venue setup crew, and shows the shape of the four fastest.

## Pick the fold by the setting, not the Pinterest board

Three questions decide it.

1. **Where will the napkin sit?** On the plate, left of the forks, in a glass, or under a ring. Each location favors a different fold.
2. **Who is folding?** Rental linens often arrive pressed and folded flat. If you want a special shape, ask the rental company, your caterer or venue whether they will fold, and whether it costs extra. Do not assume.
3. **How long between folding and dinner?** Napkins folded the morning of a summer tent event can droop in humidity. Stiff, starched napkins hold shape; soft polyester lamour napkins relax faster.

## The seven folds

<div class="table-wrap">

| Fold | Look | Speed | Holds shape on a buffet line | Best with |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flat rectangle | Clean, formal | Fastest | Excellent | Napkin rings, menus on top |
| Triangle | Classic, relaxed | Very fast | Excellent | Casual dinners, farm tables |
| Pocket fold | Tailored | Fast | Very good | Flatware or a menu card |
| Pleated fan | Dressy | Medium | Good (starch helps) | Water glass, wine glass |
| Rose | Romantic | Slow | Fair | Small wedding, sweetheart table |
| Bishop's hat | Formal, tall | Slow | Poor | Plated dinners with a stiff cloth |
| Roll with ring | Rustic | Fast | Excellent | Burlap, linen, boho tables |

</div>

### Flat rectangle and triangle

Fold the napkin in quarters for a rectangle or once diagonally for a triangle. These look plain, and that is the point. They sit neatly under a fork or on a charger and pair with a printed menu or a sprig of greenery. Nothing about them needs a special skill, so a caterer's crew can do 200 in a short window.

### Pocket fold

Fold into thirds, then fold up the bottom third, and tuck flatware or a menu into the pocket. It saves space on a round table because the fork, knife and napkin sit as one unit. For tables of 10 on a 60-inch round, that neat footprint is the reason many planners choose it.

### Pleated fan

Accordion-pleat the napkin, pinch the middle and let the ends fan out, either flat on a plate or standing in a glass. A glass fan is the classic ballroom look but needs a stiff cloth; soft napkins slump. Ask the rental company whether the napkins are pressed or starched.

### Rose, bishop's hat and the others

The rose takes several rolls and a twist and is best for a few tables, not 20. The bishop's hat is tall and photographs well, but one stray touch collapses it, and servers handling plates do touch it. If a venue offers it as a standard fold, let them do it.

## What the fold has to do around Chicago

Plated dinners at hotel ballrooms in the Loop or banquet halls in the suburbs usually have trained staff, so folding is easier to request. Tent and backyard events are different. Wind, humidity and early setup all work against tall folds, and no one on the crew may know how to fold them. Pick a flat or ring fold for a lakefront terrace or open tent, or have your napkins folded flat and add the detail on top with a sprig or a menu.

## Match the fold to the napkin and the linen

Fabric changes everything. A crisp cotton-feel napkin keeps a crease and suits pleats. Soft polyester lamour is forgiving and drapes well but folds into relaxed shapes. Heavy linen-look napkins are bulky and favor rolls and rectangles. Read our comparison of [linen fabrics](/blog/linen-fabrics-compared) before you choose, and see [napkin rings and placement](/blog/napkin-rings-and-placement) for where the napkin goes once it is folded.

Color matters as well. Napkins that contrast with the tablecloth show off the fold; a matching napkin hides it. A white napkin on an ivory cloth or a deep green napkin on an ivory cloth both give the shape something to stand against.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the easiest napkin fold for a wedding?

The flat rectangle or the triangle. Both take seconds, hold up in any weather and look polished under a ring, a menu or a sprig of greenery. They also work for any napkin fabric, which makes them the safe choice for a large guest list.

### Who folds the napkins when I rent them?

It depends on the company and the package. Many rental linens arrive pressed and folded flat, and shaped folds are either an extra service or your caterer's job. Ask your rental provider and your venue in writing before the day.

### Can I fold napkins ahead of time?

Yes, for flat folds. Folded rectangles and triangles can be done a day early and stacked in a clean box. Tall folds like a fan in a glass should be done close to service because they relax and tip over.

### What napkin fold holds silverware?

The pocket fold. Fold the napkin in thirds lengthwise, fold the bottom third up to make a pocket, then flip and tuck the ends. Place the fork and knife inside, handles up.

### Do cloth napkins look better than paper?

For a seated dinner, yes. Cloth napkins give the table weight and let you use color. Paper or printed cocktail napkins are fine for the reception hour and late-night snacks.

### What size napkin is best for folds?

A 20 x 20-inch napkin is the standard because it is large enough to fold into a pleat or pocket and still cover a lap. Smaller napkins look thin once folded.
