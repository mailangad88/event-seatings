**Quick answer**

To plan a silent auction, start sourcing items three to four months ahead, aim for roughly one item per two to three guests, and set starting bids around 40 to 50 percent of fair value. Choose paper bid sheets or mobile bidding, arrange tables so guests can circulate, close in groups, and staff checkout with volunteers. Count tables, linens and display risers before you pick a venue.

</div>

A silent auction is two events happening at once: a party and a store. If the display is cramped, guests skip it. If the bidding is confusing, they don't bid. If checkout takes an hour, they leave annoyed and remember that more than the fundraising. Planning the flow from first item to last receipt is what turns donated baskets into real money for your mission.

These are rules many nonprofits use, not guarantees. Your donors, items and crowd will drive actual results, so keep records to improve next year.

## Sourcing items that sell

Donated items fall into a few buckets: experiences such as dinners, trips and lessons, gift baskets, art and collectibles, services from local businesses, and sports or entertainment tickets. Experiences and unique items often attract more bidding than generic baskets. Ask board members and volunteers for introductions, and send a clear donation request letter with a deadline and a way to pick up items.

Track each donation in a simple sheet: donor, description, fair market value, restrictions and expiration date. Write a short, enticing description for each item and keep it consistent. Group items into categories so guests can browse by interest. Make sure you send a thank-you note and tax receipt information in line with your organization's policies, and check with your accountant on language.

## Bidding formats: paper or mobile

Paper bid sheets are simple and tactile, and they work without devices or Wi-Fi, but they take space, need monitoring and require manual checkout. Mobile bidding lets guests bid from their phones, get outbid alerts and pay by card, but it needs reliable cell or Wi-Fi coverage and a plan for guests who prefer paper. Some events offer both.

Whichever you use, set clear rules: opening bids, increments, buy-it-now prices and closing times. Close the auction in sections, a few tables at a time, so there is no stampede at the end and winners are announced steadily. Post the closing times near each table.

## Pricing and display

Set a minimum bid between 40 and 50 percent of fair value so guests feel they're getting a deal while the organization still raises something. Use small risers and easels to give items height, and print large clear item cards with a title, description, value and starting bid. Light the tables well, and leave at least four to five feet in front of them so guests can walk and stop without blocking the path. ## Table needs by guest count

This table gives rough starting counts for a silent auction. Adjust for the size of your items and the width of your room.

|---|---|---|---|

Larger items such as framed art or a weekend trip card may use less table space than baskets. Keep a few extra tables on hand.

## Silent auction steps

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Set a revenue goal.</strong> Know how much you expect from the auction versus tickets.</li>
<li><strong>Solicit items.</strong> Start early and track every donation.</li>
<li><strong>Choose the bidding format.</strong> Paper, mobile or both, with clear rules.</li>
<li><strong>Plan the layout.</strong> Map tables, aisles, lighting and checkout.</li>
<li><strong>Recruit volunteers.</strong> Assign people to monitor tables, close sections and run checkout. See [volunteer coordination](/blog/volunteer-coordination-for-nonprofit-events).</li>
<li><strong>Close and settle.</strong> Announce winners, process payments and hand over items.</li>
<li><strong>Thank donors and bidders.</strong> Send notes and results within a week.</li>
</ol>

## Chicago-area notes

Chicago-area auctions often run in banquet halls, hotels, parish halls and school gyms. Ask the venue how early you can set up tables and whether they provide risers. If your event is a school fundraiser, see our [school fundraiser rentals guide](/blog/school-fundraiser-rentals-chicago). Check with your village or the state about raffle and gaming rules, since a raffle or other games of chance may require a license, and ask your attorney or the relevant office rather than guessing. Winter auctions need coat check, and downtown parking can slow guest arrival, so build a longer cocktail hour into the schedule.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I plan a silent auction?

Set a revenue goal, solicit items three to four months ahead, choose a bidding format, plan the layout and checkout, recruit volunteers, and thank donors afterward.

### How many items do I need for a silent auction?

A common target is about one item for every two to three guests. A 150-guest event might have 50 to 75 items.

### How do I set starting bids?

Many organizations start at 40 to 50 percent of fair market value and use increments of 10 to 15 percent of value. Adjust based on your crowd.

### Should I use paper bid sheets or mobile bidding?

Paper is simple and works without devices. Mobile bidding adds alerts and easy payment but needs good connectivity. Some events offer both.

### How long should a silent auction run?

Most run during cocktails and dinner, usually 90 minutes to two hours, with sections closing in stages.

### How many tables do I need for a silent auction?

Plan about one 6-foot table for every five to eight items, plus one or two checkout tables per 150 guests.

### What are the best silent auction items?

Experiences, unique items and local services often draw strong bidding. Mix price levels so everyone can participate.
