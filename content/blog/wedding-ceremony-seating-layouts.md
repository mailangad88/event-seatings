**Quick answer**

The five common wedding ceremony layouts are center aisle, two side aisles, semicircle (or fan), chevron (angled rows) and in the round. A center aisle fits narrow rooms and traditional ceremonies. A semicircle puts more guests near the couple. In the round suits small guest counts under about 60. Plan roughly 8 square feet per guest, with 36 inches between rows and a 4 to 6 foot aisle.

</div>

The chair layout shapes the ceremony more than any decor. It decides who sees the vows, how the processional feels and how fast guests exit to cocktail hour. Here is how each layout works for Chicago-area rooms, from long banquet halls to square suburban lawns.

## Center aisle

Two blocks of chairs with a single aisle down the middle. It is the classic, it fits long narrow rooms and tents, and it gives the processional a clear line. The downside: guests seated far from the center see the couple at an angle. Use it for 50 to 200 guests. Rows of 5 to 8 chairs per side keep it easy to reach seats.

## Two side aisles

Three blocks with two aisles. The middle block faces the couple directly and the aisles stay near the walls. It works in wide rooms and halls where a single aisle would leave the sides empty. Wedding parties can split on the aisles, and each walks down its own.

## Semicircle (fan)

Curved rows fan around the focal point. The couple is closer to more guests, and the whole group feels intimate. It takes more floor area than straight rows and needs more careful chair counts per arc. It suits lawns and open ballrooms, not narrow rooms.

## Chevron

Rows angle toward the center to form a V. It keeps the aisle tidy, opens the view and fits trapezoid spaces. The layout needs a clear floor plan and a rental crew that can align the angles.

## In the round

Guests sit in a ring around the couple. It works for small ceremonies, under about 60 guests, and for backyard or living room vows. Because everyone sees someone's back, the couple usually turns to each section during the ceremony.

## Spacing numbers

- **Chair to chair:** 18 to 20 inches center to center in a row.
- **Row to row:** 36 inches, so guests can slide past knees and you have room for chair backs.
- **Aisle width:** 4 to 6 feet.
- **Row length:** no more than 8 to 10 chairs without an aisle or break, or guests have to climb over each other.

Multiply to get a footprint: 100 guests at 10 rows of 10 take about 20 feet wide plus aisle, and 30 feet deep. See [how many chairs do I need](/blog/how-many-chairs-do-i-need) for the full count.

Chicago halls and ballrooms often have a pillar or two; sketch them so you do not seat 8 guests behind one. A river-view or lakefront venue may want the layout to frame the water instead of the room.

Chair choice matters for the look from the aisle. Clear [ghost chairs](/chairs/ghost) keep sight lines open, and [garden folding chairs](/chairs/garden-folding) are the lightest to move. For the flip to your reception, read [ceremony vs reception chairs](/blog/ceremony-vs-reception-chairs).

## Reserved seating and the first rows

Decide early whether you will mark seats. Most couples reserve only the first two rows for parents, grandparents and siblings, and leave the rest open. Simple signs on the chair backs, a ribbon, or a small printed card work. Avoid a full assigned-seat ceremony unless the guest list is small, because it slows arrivals and creates awkward gaps.

Tell ushers which seats are reserved and where late guests should sit. A pair of ushers at the back, one per side, makes a bigger difference to the flow than any change in the chair layout.

## Lighting and sound change the layout

If the ceremony is in the evening, add lighting to the aisle and focal point, and keep chairs back from the edge of any tent or building. Speakers belong at the front, pointed back toward the audience, never in the middle of the aisle. A semicircle needs two speakers to reach the curved ends, while a center aisle in a long room may need a third near the back rows.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many chairs per row at a wedding ceremony?

Five to eight chairs per row on each side of the aisle is typical. More than 10 makes guests climb over each other. If you need a longer row, add a second aisle.

### How much space do I need per guest at a ceremony?

Plan about 8 square feet per guest including aisles, as a rule of thumb. Wider chairs or wide aisles need more.

### How wide should the wedding aisle be?

Between 4 and 6 feet. This lets two people walk together, and allows for a mobility device.

### What is a chevron ceremony layout?

Chevron seating angles the rows toward the center in a V shape, so more guests face the couple directly. It needs careful measuring but looks open and modern.

### What layout is best for a small ceremony?

For under 60 guests, the in-the-round or semicircle layout feels more intimate than straight rows. A short center aisle also works if the room is narrow.

### Should family sit in the front row?

Traditionally yes, with parents and grandparents in the first one or two rows. Reserve those chairs with a sign or ribbon so late arrivals do not take them.
