Passover seders are meant to be shared, and many families grow from 12 to 40 guests without noticing. The real problem is the table: a seder is a conversation, not a plated dinner, and the leader needs to be heard from one end to the other. Add Haggadahs, wine, matzah, a seder plate and a menu that follows Passover rules, and the planning gets complicated fast. Here is a calm way to set it up.

## What a seder involves

The seder is a ritual meal on the first night of Passover, and in many communities outside Israel, the second night too. Families read the story of the Exodus from the Haggadah, eat symbolic foods, drink four cups of wine or grape juice, and share a meal. The seder plate holds items such as a roasted shank bone (zeroa), a roasted egg (beitzah), bitter herbs (maror), charoset, and a green vegetable (karpas), and some families add a plate item such as an orange or a different symbol. Matzah is placed on the table, usually covered, and many families set a cup for Elijah and sometimes a cup for Miriam.

Customs vary. Orthodox, Conservative, Reform and secular families, and Sephardic, Ashkenazi and Mizrahi traditions each have their own foods and melodies. Some families keep strict kosher for Passover rules, which affect dishes, pans, ingredients and even rented serveware. If this matters to you, ask your rabbi or community what applies, and tell rental and catering vendors in advance.

## Counting tables, chairs and place settings

Allow roughly 24 inches of table width per guest so that people can lean back on cushions and pass dishes. An 8 foot table seats about 6 to 8 people on both sides, but for a seder 6 to 7 per table is more comfortable because you need space for the Haggadah, wine cups and plates. Hosts who need more seats often join tables end to end so the group feels like one family. Add a kids table within sight of the main one. For round tables, 60 inch rounds seat 8 to 10; see [round table sizes](/blog/round-table-sizes-and-seating-48-60-66-72-inch). Do not forget a table for the buffet or serving dishes, and a drink station for water.

Tell guests if they will need to bring their own Haggadah, and consider buying matching copies for the whole group. Having the same page numbers and translation makes the reading smoother.

## Place setting checklist

- A plate, fork, knife and water glass for each guest.
- A wine or grape juice cup for each guest, plus Elijah's cup.
- A Haggadah and, if you like, a cushion for leaning.
- Matzah on a covered plate, a bowl of salt water, charoset and maror on each table.
- Candles for the start of the holiday.
- A hand-washing bowl, pitcher and towel for ritual washing.

If you serve food from a hotel or caterer, ask about kosher for Passover options, and whether the rented dishes need to be kosher for Passover. For rented items, see [dinnerware sizes for buffet and plated meals](/blog/dinnerware-sizes-buffet-plated-family-style).

## Keeping the evening on track

A seder can run long. Pick a Haggadah length that fits your group, and agree on whether to read everything or highlight parts. Mix reading, singing and discussion. Give kids a job, and offer snacks such as parsley, boiled egg or matzah during the service so no one is cranky before the meal. Ask a relative to watch the oven and food timing. Keep a quiet place for guests who need a break.

If you are hosting a community seder at a hall or synagogue, plan a flow for serving a larger meal after the service: buffet lines, table service or family-style platters. For caterers, ask early about Passover availability, since it is a busy season.

## Chicago-area notes

Passover falls in spring, usually March or April, and Chicago weather can be anything from snow to warm sun. Most seders are indoors at a home, a synagogue hall or a banquet room, but a spring backyard seder under a tent is possible if you add heat and sidewalls. Community seders are held by many congregations around the city and suburbs, and registration fills up early. If you host a larger event in a rented space, check parking, coats and the kitchen rules for kosher for Passover cooking. Check with your own congregation for local resources.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many people can you seat at a seder?

A seder can be any size. A home table often seats 8 to 16, while joined tables or a hall can seat 40 to 100 or more. Allow about 24 inches of table width per guest so people can read and pass dishes.

### What do you need on a seder table?

Each seat needs a plate, utensils, a wine or juice cup and a Haggadah. The table needs a seder plate, matzah, salt water, and a cup for Elijah. Candles and a hand-washing setup are also common.

### How long does a seder last?

The service portion commonly runs one to three hours before the meal, depending on the Haggadah and how much discussion there is. Families set their own pace.

### Can I rent dishes for a kosher for Passover seder?

Some families do, but rules vary. Ask your rabbi what is acceptable and ask the rental company if they have Passover-friendly serveware. Disposable or newly purchased dishes are an alternative.

### How much wine do I need for a seder?

Traditionally four cups per person, though the cups are small and many people drink grape juice. Plan roughly one bottle for every 4 to 6 guests and buy extra juice.

### How do I seat a large seder so everyone can hear?

Join tables into one long table or a U shape so the leader is visible, or use a few rounds near the leader. Keep the room quiet and consider a microphone for 40 or more guests.

### Do I need a seder plate for each table?

Many hosts put one in the center of each table or section so everyone can see and reach it. It is also common to have a single plate and pass items around.

### What if guests have different dietary needs?

Ask in advance about vegetarian, gluten-free, allergy or kosher needs. Keep a small separate menu and label foods, and ask relatives to bring dishes that fit.
