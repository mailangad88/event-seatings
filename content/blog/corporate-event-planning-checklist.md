**Quick answer**

A corporate event checklist starts with the goal, the headcount and the budget, then moves to venue, catering, program, rentals and communications. Plan on about 12 weeks for a 100 to 200 person event, and longer for anything with travel or a keynote speaker. Lock the date and venue first, confirm vendors by week eight, and spend the last two weeks on headcounts, run of show and day-of logistics.

</div>

The hard part of a corporate event is rarely any single task. It is that fifteen small decisions depend on each other, and the person running it also has a day job. The venue needs a headcount, the caterer needs a headcount, the rental order needs a headcount, and nobody can give you a final headcount until invitations have been out for weeks. A checklist in the right order keeps those dependencies from catching you late.

These are planning habits, not guarantees. Your attendance, budget split and lead times will differ with the type of event, the season and whether people are traveling in. Treat the numbers as a starting frame and adjust once you have real quotes.

## Start with the goal, not the venue

Before you open a single venue website, write one sentence that finishes the phrase, "This event succeeds if..." It might be that the sales team leaves with the new product story, that clients feel thanked, or that new hires meet their peers. That sentence decides almost everything else: how formal the room feels, how long the program runs, whether you need a stage, and how much you spend per head.

Then list the fixed facts. Who is the audience, and how many people? Who approves the budget? Is there a date the event cannot move from, such as a fiscal quarter close or a product launch? Which executives need to speak or attend? Getting these answers in writing in week one prevents the most common rework, which is a venue booked before leadership agreed on the guest count.

## What the checklist covers

A useful way to organize the work is by workstream, each with one owner. The big ones are budget, venue and date, food and beverage, program and speakers, invitations and registration, rentals and decor, audiovisual, transportation and parking, and day-of staffing. If your team is small, one person may own several, but write the names down anyway. Shared ownership usually means no ownership.

Within each workstream, keep a short list of decisions and a due date for each. For rentals, that list is simple: seating style, table count, linens, bar and buffet equipment, lounge or registration furniture, and any tent or stage. Our guide on [how many chairs you need](/blog/how-many-chairs-do-i-need) shows how to count seating, and the [conference seating styles guide](/blog/conference-seating-styles-theater-classroom-banquet-u-shape) helps you choose between theater, classroom and banquet layouts.

## Budget before you book

Put a total number on paper before you collect quotes, and leave a contingency line. Many planners hold back 10 to 15 percent for the surprises: a larger headcount, a last-minute speaker request, an extra hour of overtime. A rough per-person figure helps too, and our guide to [corporate event budget per person](/blog/corporate-event-budget-per-person) breaks the split down by category.

Food and beverage and the venue are usually the largest shares, but rentals, audiovisual and staffing add up faster than people expect. Ask every vendor what is included and what is billed separately, such as delivery, setup, service charges and taxes, so you compare totals instead of headlines.

## Checklist by weeks out

Here is the same plan as a table you can copy into your project tracker.

<div class="table-wrap">

| When | Key tasks | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| 10 to 12 weeks | Goal, budget, guest count, date options | Event lead |
| 8 to 10 weeks | Book venue, catering and AV; request rental quotes | Event lead |
| 6 to 8 weeks | Speakers confirmed, program drafted, invitations designed | Program owner |
| 4 to 6 weeks | Invitations out, registration open, rental order placed | Registration owner |
| 2 to 3 weeks | Reminders, dietary notes, seating plan, signage | Event lead |
| 1 week | Final headcount, run of show shared, vendor timing confirmed | Event lead |
| Day of | Load-in, walkthrough, check-in, program, load-out | Day-of lead |

</div>

Adjust the weeks if your event is smaller or simpler. A 40-person leadership dinner may need only half the time, while a 300-person conference with out-of-town guests needs more.

## Order of operations

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Define success.</strong> Write the goal, audience and must-have moments in one paragraph.</li>
<li><strong>Set the budget and date.</strong> Get approval for a total and two or three possible dates.</li>
<li><strong>Book the venue and caterer.</strong> Confirm what the venue includes so you rent only what is missing.</li>
<li><strong>Build the program.</strong> Draft a run of show with times, speakers and transitions. See our [run of show guide](/blog/event-run-of-show-how-to-write-one).</li>
<li><strong>Open registration.</strong> Send invitations and collect dietary needs with the RSVP.</li>
<li><strong>Order rentals.</strong> Use your confirmed layout to count chairs, tables, linens and serving pieces.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm everything.</strong> Reconfirm vendor arrival windows and share the final schedule.</li>
</ol>

## Chicago-area notes

Chicago planners should think about the season and the street. Winter events need coat check space and a plan for guests arriving in snow, while summer events downtown compete with festivals and road closures. Loop and River North loading docks often have tight windows, so ask your venue about delivery times and read our [Chicago delivery and loading zone guide](/blog/chicago-event-delivery-parking-loading-zones). High-rise venues may ask for a certificate of insurance from every vendor, which our guide to [corporate event COI and vendor requirements](/blog/corporate-event-coi-and-vendor-requirements) explains. In the suburbs, check with your village or park district about permits for outdoor tents or amplified sound, and ask about parking capacity before you invite a crowd.

## Frequently asked questions

### How far in advance should I plan a corporate event?

For a 100 to 200 person event, about 12 weeks is a comfortable window. Smaller dinners can come together in four to six weeks, while conferences with travel or a headline speaker often need six months or more. Popular Chicago dates, especially in December, book earlier.

### What should be on a corporate event checklist?

Cover goal and audience, budget, date and venue, catering, program and speakers, invitations and registration, audiovisual, rentals and decor, transportation and parking, and day-of staffing. Give each item an owner and a due date.

### How do I set a budget for a corporate event?

Start with a total approved by whoever pays, then divide it by expected attendees to get a per-person figure. Hold back 10 to 15 percent for surprises. Get written quotes before you commit, since costs vary by season and venue.

### Who should be on the event planning team?

Name one overall lead and one owner for each major workstream, such as food, program, registration and logistics. A small team of three to five people works for most events. Add an executive sponsor who can make quick decisions.

### What are the most common corporate event planning mistakes?

Booking a venue before agreeing on headcount, forgetting dietary needs, underestimating setup time and having no day-of owner are the big ones. A written run of show and a final vendor confirmation call prevent most of them.

### How many people should I invite to reach my target attendance?

Many planners invite more than they need because not everyone who registers attends. The rate varies by event type and whether tickets are free, so track your own history if you have it.

### Do I need to rent furniture for a corporate event?

Often yes, even at a venue. Venues may supply basic tables and chairs but not enough for your layout, or not the style you want. Ask what is included, then request a quote for the gap.
