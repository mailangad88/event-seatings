**Quick answer**

For good night photos, give the photographer enough soft, warm light at faces, keep light sources the same color temperature (2700K to 3000K is a common choice), avoid bare bulbs behind subjects, and leave a few feet of space for a flash bounce. Walk the venue at dusk with your photographer and plan a portrait spot lit from the front and side, not from overhead or behind.

</div>

Night events look romantic to the eye and difficult to a camera. The eye adjusts to dim rooms. The camera does not, so it raises its sensitivity and the photos turn grainy and yellow-green. Good photos at night need a lighting plan, and the plan is more about balance and placement than raw brightness. This guide covers the basics a planner can control.

## What a camera needs

Cameras need three things at night: enough light on faces, even color, and no extreme contrast. Many photographers use an on-camera or off-camera flash to supplement ambient light, but flash looks best when the room has some glow of its own. A completely dark room with a single flash gives a harsh, flat look with a black background.

The three issues to solve:

1. **Too dark overall.** Add warm ambient light in the room: string lights, uplights, candles or lamps.
2. **Backlit faces.** A bright window, spotlight or bar behind the subject turns faces into silhouettes. Move the subject or the light.
3. **Mixed colors.** A room with warm bulbs, cool LEDs, and colored uplights confuses white balance, which means skin color changes from shot to shot. Pick one family of whites. See [lighting color temperature](/blog/lighting-color-temperature-events).

## Plan these spots with the photographer

Ask the photographer what they need before the event, and walk the room at the same time of day if you can.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Spot | What to plan | Problem to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Ceremony | Even light on the couple, soft background | Sun behind them, no light after dusk |
| Portrait corner | A wall or hedge with warm lights, 8 to 10 feet of space | Overhead only light |
| Head table | Soft light on faces, pin spot on flowers | Candles only |
| Cake cutting | A bright area, not a spotlight | Dark corner |
| Dance floor | Moving lights, but steady white too | Only colored strobes |
| Exit | Light the path | Pitch black |

</div>

## Match the light to sunset

A summer evening in Chicago has the sun setting after 8 p.m. until early August, so a 7 p.m. ceremony still has daylight and the photos will be bright. In late fall and winter, sunset arrives as early as 4:19 p.m. in early December, so even an afternoon event ends in the dark. Check the exact time for your date and read [winter event lighting in Chicago](/blog/winter-event-lighting-chicago-sunset). Golden hour, the hour before sunset, produces the softest natural light for portraits. Many couples schedule portraits during it.

## Light types that photograph well

- **Warm LED string lights and lanterns** add glow and sparkle in the background.
- **Soft uplighting** brings walls out of the dark; see [how many uplights](/blog/how-many-uplights-for-a-wedding).
- **Pin spots** make centerpieces stand out; see [pin spot lighting](/blog/pin-spot-lighting-centerpieces).
- **Large soft sources** such as paper lanterns or a diffused lamp flatter faces.
- **Candles or LED candles** add sparkle and warmth, but do not provide enough light alone. See [candles at venues](/blog/candles-at-venues-fire-code-rules).

What to limit:

- **Colored LED uplights near the portrait spot.** They can tint skin.
- **Fluorescent or cool white work lights.** They make people look gray.
- **Spotlights from behind.** They cause flare and silhouettes.

## The flash question

Most photographers use flash. Flash works best when it can bounce off a nearby ceiling or wall. In a tent with a high peak or a black ceiling, bouncing is harder, so the photographer may use off-camera lights on stands. Ask whether the photographer needs a power outlet or space for a stand. Some venues restrict stands, so check the policy.

## Don't forget guest photos

Guests take phone photos too. Phones do best with moderate, warm light on faces. Avoid the pitch-dark lounge. A few lamps or lanterns near the bar and photo areas help every camera in the room.

## Chicago notes

Chicago skyline backdrops are popular. A downtown rooftop gives great evening views, but it also introduces bright city light behind your guests. Plan the portrait position so the skyline sits behind the couple but the faces get warm light from the front. Venues that limit flash or require approved vendors should say so in their rules.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much light do I need for night photos?

Enough that faces are clearly visible and warm, without harsh shadows. Your photographer will supplement with flash. Walk the room together at dusk to judge it.

### What color light is best for photos?

Warm white at 2700K to 3000K is common and flattering. Keep sources consistent so skin tones stay steady.

### Should the room be dark for dancing?

Dim, but not dark. Keep steady white light for photos along with color effects.

### Will colored uplights ruin my photos?

They can tint skin and white dresses. Keep them to walls and away from the portrait area.

### Do I need extra lighting for the photographer?

Ask. Many bring their own, but need outlets and space for stands.

### Does sunset time matter for photos?

Yes. Plan portraits around golden hour and the ceremony before the light fades.
