**Quick answer**

A pin spot is a small, narrow-beam fixture that puts a tight circle of light on one centerpiece. Plan one pin spot per table, mounted 12 to 20 feet above it, aimed straight down at the arrangement. The beam should be about the width of the centerpiece, usually 12 to 24 inches. Rigging is the real cost: you need a truss, ceiling grid or stands, and every fixture needs power and aiming time.

</div>

Walk into a good ballroom reception and you will notice the tables seem to glow while the room around them is soft and dim. That effect comes from pin spots. They pull the eye to the flowers, the candles and the place settings, and they make photos look richer. They are also one of the first items cut from a lighting budget, because they take planning. This guide shows how they work and what to arrange before you hire.

## What a pin spot does

A pin spot is a spotlight with a very narrow beam, often 5 to 10 degrees. Because the beam is narrow, the light lands only on the centerpiece instead of spilling across the table, the guests and the linens. The darker surroundings make the lit object seem brighter.

## How many do you need?

One per table is the standard. For a long banquet table, use one per 4 to 6 feet. For a sweetheart table, a slightly wider beam (a "mini spot") covers the table and the couple.

|---|---|---|---|
| Ballroom with ceiling rigging | 14 to 20 ft | 12 to 24 in | Best control |
| Truss over a tent | 10 to 14 ft | 12 to 18 in | Needs truss rental |
| Barn with beams | 12 to 16 ft | 12 to 24 in | Check beam capacity |
| Low-ceiling banquet hall | 9 to 10 ft | 10 to 14 in | Beams hit heads when guests stand |

## Rigging options

Pin spots are only useful if they have somewhere to hang. Ask which of these your venue allows:

- **Ceiling grid or house rigging points.** Many hotel ballrooms have approved points. Some require the hotel's in-house crew.
- **Truss and stands.** A freestanding truss ring or box can go in tents or barns. It adds cost and floor space.
- **Fixtures clamped to beams.** Works in barns and lofts when the beam is rated to hold the load. The venue should tell you.
- **Floor stands.** Tall tripods can aim up at an angle, but they get in the way and are rarely used for every table.

Always ask for the venue's rigging policy in writing. Some require licensed riggers, and some prohibit hanging anything from the ceiling.

## Aiming and testing

Aiming takes time. A crew member sets up each fixture, focuses the beam and checks for glare from the head table and the dance floor. Plan 20 to 30 minutes per 10 tables for an experienced crew, and do not move the tables after aiming. If a table shifts a foot, the beam can miss the centerpiece. For that reason, set the final floor plan first. Our [layout guides](/blog/topic/layouts-and-floor-plans) help you lock it.

## Design choices that work with pin spots

- **Reflective surfaces glow.** Glassware, mirror plates, metallic candlesticks and white flowers catch the light. Matte dark objects absorb it.
- **Keep height under control.** A very tall centerpiece may be lit from the top only, leaving the lower half dark. Low arrangements pair better, as does a tall one with a lit base.
- **Think about faces.** The lit area should not hit guests' eyes. A beam straight down with a flat linen is gentle.
- **Warm light flatters food.** See [lighting color temperature](/blog/lighting-color-temperature-events) for the range most planners choose.

Pin spots also look wonderful with a [monogram or gobo](/blog/monogram-gobo-projection) on the dance floor, and they work together with [candles at venues](/blog/candles-at-venues-fire-code-rules) when candles are allowed.

## Power

A pin spot draws modest power, but an entire room of them needs planned circuits. Ask your vendor for the total wattage and check [your event's power needs](/blog/event-power-needs-checklist).

## Chicago notes

Many downtown Chicago hotels and event spaces use in-house AV and rigging crews, and outside vendors may need a certificate of insurance. Suburban banquet halls vary widely. Ask the venue directly.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a pin spot?

A pin spot is a small spotlight with a very narrow beam. It places a circle of light on a single object, like a centerpiece, and leaves the surroundings dim.

### How many pin spots do I need for a wedding?

One per table is standard, plus extras for the cake, the sweetheart table and any signage. A 15-table reception usually needs 18 to 22.

### How high should a pin spot be?

Hang it 12 to 20 feet above the table. Higher fixtures keep the beam narrow and the glare away from guests' eyes.

### Do pin spots need a truss?

Often yes. If the venue has no ceiling rigging points, you need a truss or beam clamps, which add cost.

### Can I use pin spots in a tent?

Yes, with a truss or lighting stands that the tent company approves. Never hang fixtures from tent poles or fabric without permission.

### Do pin spots work with candles?

Yes. The glass, metal and flame catch the light well. Confirm the venue allows candles first.

### What happens if guests move the table?

The beam misses the centerpiece. Mark table positions on the floor and ask guests not to shift them.
