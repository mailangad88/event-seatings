Give your photographer or helper a short list of must-have shots: the room before guests arrive, the guest of honor, key family and group photos, food and decor details, the toast, the cake moment, candids of guests and a send-off. Yet many hosts realize afterward that no one took the group photo with grandma, the room was never shot before guests arrived or the best moment was missed because the photographer was in the wrong place. A shot list solves it. It also helps if a friend, not a pro, is holding the camera. Here is a list you can copy and adapt.

## Before guests arrive

Capture the room while it is clean: wide shots of the space, table settings, centerpieces, signs, the cake or dessert table and the entrance. If you have rentals, those chairs and linens will look their best now. Lighting is often best an hour before the event for outdoor settings. See [lighting for night event photos](/blog/lighting-for-night-event-photos) if your event runs late.

## Arrivals and candids

Capture people greeting each other, hugging and laughing. Candids of guests are usually the most loved photos. Ask your photographer to shoot from different angles and to find the quiet moments, such as a grandparent watching a child.

## The guest of honor

Plan some portraits: solo, with a partner, with parents and with close friends. For a graduation, birthday or retirement, a prop or a meaningful object can help. A [photo backdrop](/blog/photo-backdrop-ideas-for-weddings-and-parties) gives a defined spot. See also [corporate event photo backdrop guide](/blog/corporate-event-photo-backdrop-guide).

## Group photos

Write a list of group photos with names, in order, and appoint someone who knows the family to gather people. Common groups: immediate family, each side of the family, grandparents with grandchildren, friend groups, coworkers and the whole group. Keep the session to ten to fifteen minutes. Schedule it after the toast or cake, when guests are present and sober.

## Details

Shoot the food, drinks, cake, signs, flowers and favors. If you spent money on decor, capture it. Take photos of rentals in place, since guests rarely photograph them. Close-ups of place settings and centerpieces are good for sharing.

## Moments

Plan the key moments: the toast, the first dance or speech, the cake cutting, any games, and the send-off. Put these on your timeline so the photographer is in position. See the [four-hour party timeline](/blog/four-hour-party-timeline).

## Brief the photographer

Share the list, the timeline, the location, the guest of honor's name and any family sensitivities, such as divorced parents who should not be photographed together. Tell them what you want to receive and when. Ask about delivery time, usage rights and their backup plan. If you hire one, see [how to hire event vendors](/blog/how-to-hire-event-vendors) and [event photographer cost and hiring](/blog/event-photographer-cost-and-hiring).

## If a friend is shooting

Ask for a short list, share a quick lighting tip and give them a role so they can still enjoy the party. Use a second phone or a shared album so guests can upload their photos. A QR code on tables helps.

## Chicago-area notes

Chicago-area light changes fast with the seasons. Summer sunsets come late, winter afternoons go dark by dinner, and lakefront or rooftop spots can be windy. Plan outdoor group photos earlier in the day or near golden hour. Some parks, forest preserves, museums and downtown spaces have rules or permits for professional photography, so ask your venue or park district. Indoor ballrooms may have low light, so ask your photographer about lighting. Have a rain spot ready for group photos.

## Frequently asked questions

### What photos should I take at an event?

Capture the empty room, decor details, arrivals, the guest of honor, group photos, food, the toast, the cake moment and candids. Add a send-off shot. Keep the list short enough to follow.

### How many group photos should I plan?

Five to eight is plenty for most events, such as immediate family, each side of the family, friend groups and everyone together. More takes time and tires guests.

### When should group photos be taken?

After a toast or cake, when most guests are present. Avoid doing them during food service. Keep it to ten to fifteen minutes.

### How do I brief an event photographer?

Share the timeline, location, shot list, key names and any sensitivities. Tell them how you want to receive photos and when. Confirm arrival and end times.

### Do I need a professional photographer?

Not always. A friend with a good phone can capture many events. For weddings, large events and photos you will use for years, many hosts prefer a professional.

### How can guests share their photos?

Set up a shared album and display a QR code on tables or signs. Remind guests in the invitation or toast. Ask them to add photos after the event.

### What time is best for outdoor photos?

Soft light early in the morning or an hour before sunset is usually flattering. Midday sun is harsh, so use shade or a tent. Check the forecast.
