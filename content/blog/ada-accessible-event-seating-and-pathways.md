**Quick answer**

Plan at least a 36-inch-wide clear path (60 inches where two wheelchairs must pass) to every table, a wheelchair space of at least 36 x 48 inches at each seating area, and a 60-inch turning circle at key points. Spread accessible spaces around the room, not in one corner. Check your venue's own accessibility features and your local rules.

</div>

This is practical planning advice, not legal advice. Venues in Chicago and the suburbs are generally responsible for accessibility in their buildings, but the layout inside the room is yours, and a good layout makes the difference between a guest who enjoys the event and one who is stuck near the exit. The rental order matters too, since chair counts, table sizes and spacing all change who can move through the room.

## The key measurements

The federal ADA Standards use these dimensions for wheelchair spaces in assembly areas: a space at least 36 inches wide and 48 inches deep when a chair can enter from the front or back, and 60 inches deep if it can only be entered from the side. Accessible routes are generally at least 36 inches wide and a wheelchair turning space is a 60-inch circle. Those are federal baselines; your venue or local code may add to them.

| Element | Planning number | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Clear path | 36 in minimum | Between tables, chairs pulled out, to restrooms |
| Two-way path | 60 in | Where two wheelchairs, or a chair and a server, pass |
| Wheelchair space | 36 x 48 in | Add companion seat next to it |
| Turning space | 60 in circle | At entrance, near bars, at the head of a dead-end aisle |
| Table height | About 28 to 34 in under-surface knee clearance | Rental banquet tables are 30 in tall; confirm knee clearance |

## Where to place accessible spaces

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Spread them out.</strong> Offer wheelchair spaces at several tables, not only at the back.</li>
<li><strong>Pair with a companion seat.</strong> Next to every space, so guests can sit with family.</li>
<li><strong>Near the route, not blocking it.</strong> Close to a main aisle but outside the traffic.</li>
<li><strong>Near the restrooms.</strong> Where possible, with a clear route.</li>
<li><strong>Leave removable chairs.</strong> Take away a chair at a table instead of inventing a new table.</li>
</ol>

A common approach: when guests tell you they use a wheelchair, remove one chair from their assigned table before the event so the guest rolls up to the table instead of sitting in an aisle. Ask on the RSVP card whether anyone needs accessible seating.

## Aisles between tables

If chairs are pulled out, a table that looks 5 feet from the next might be less than 3 feet of usable path. When tables are 10 feet apart (center to center) and 72 inches wide, the gap is 4 feet, minus chairs. To keep a 36-inch path, plan at least 5 feet between table edges, or push tables apart where the route runs. The sizing in [round table sizes and seating](/blog/round-table-sizes-and-seating-48-60-66-72-inch) helps you check, and our [reception layout mistakes guide](/blog/reception-layout-mistakes-to-avoid) covers spacing errors.

## Outdoor events

Grass, gravel and sand make rolling difficult. Use a firm path from the parking or drop-off to the seating, such as boards or mats, and check the surface under the accessible seating. See [chairs on grass, sand and gravel](/blog/chairs-on-grass-sand-gravel). Tents with a threshold need a ramp or a level entry. Also think about shade, weather and restrooms. Our [ADA restroom considerations](/blog/ada-restroom-considerations-for-events) covers portable options.

## Seating that supports guests

Not every guest who needs a good seat uses a wheelchair. Older guests and people with limited mobility often prefer a chair with arms, a firmer seat or a spot near an exit. See [comfortable chairs for older guests](/blog/comfortable-chairs-for-older-guests). Reserve a few tables near the restrooms and the exits and assign guests there ahead of time.

## What to ask your venue

Every venue in the Chicago area is different. Ask: is the entrance step-free, is there an accessible restroom on the same floor, are doors at least 32 inches wide, and where do accessible parking spaces sit? If you have any doubt, ask for a walkthrough. For events on public property such as a park, the park district or city can tell you about accessible routes. We do not offer legal advice; use the venue and local authorities to confirm.

## Chairs and tables for access

Choose chairs without sled bases that catch wheelchair footplates, and make sure chairs can be removed quickly. A [garden folding chair](/chairs/garden-folding) is light and easy to move. Tables with a pedestal base leave more knee room than trestle legs. Mention accessibility in your [quote request](/quote) so we can plan with it.

## Frequently asked questions

### How wide should the path be between tables for a wheelchair?

At least 36 inches clear, measured with chairs pulled out. Plan 5 feet between table edges to be safe.

### How much space does a wheelchair need at a table?

A space at least 36 inches wide and 48 inches deep, with a companion seat beside it.

### What is a 60-inch turning circle?

It is the open floor a wheelchair user needs to make a full turn. Plan one at entrances, bars and the ends of aisles.

### Where should wheelchair seating go at a wedding?

Spread across the room, close to a main route, with companion seats, rather than in a single back corner.

### Are rental banquet tables accessible?

Standard 30-inch tables often work, but check knee clearance. Pedestal tables and rounds without cross braces are easier.

### Who is responsible for accessibility at a private event?

Venues are generally responsible for their buildings. As a host you should plan your layout. Check with your venue and local authorities for the rules that apply.
