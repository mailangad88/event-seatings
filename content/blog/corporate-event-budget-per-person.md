**Quick answer**

Per-person cost for a corporate event varies widely, so start from your total approved budget and divide by expected attendees, then split it by category. Many planners put roughly 40 to 50 percent into food and beverage, 15 to 25 percent into the venue, and the rest into rentals, audiovisual, staffing and a contingency. Get written quotes in your area, since costs shift with season and neighborhood.

</div>

Someone asks, "What does a corporate event cost per person?" and the honest answer is that the number depends on what you are buying. A cocktail hour in a rented room is not a plated dinner with a keynote. What helps is a method: start with the total you can spend, divide by realistic attendance, and decide where each dollar goes before you fall in love with a venue.

## Budget rules of thumb

<div class="stat-grid">
<div><strong>40 to 50%</strong><span>Common share for food and beverage</span></div>
<div><strong>15 to 25%</strong><span>Common share for venue and rentals</span></div>
<div><strong>10 to 15%</strong><span>Contingency many planners hold</span></div>

</div>

Percentages are typical planning starting points, not promises about your event. Verify service charges, gratuity and taxes in each quote, because they change the real per-person number more than the menu price.

## Why per-person math can mislead

Per-person figures are handy for comparison, but they hide fixed costs. A venue rental, a stage and an AV package cost the same whether 80 or 120 people attend, so the more guests, the lower the per-person number. Food and beverage, and rentals like chairs and linens, scale directly with headcount.

That is why a fair budget separates fixed costs from variable costs. Add up the fixed ones first: venue, AV, entertainment, decor, permits. Subtract them from the total. Divide what is left by expected guests to see what you can spend per head on food, drink and seating. If that number looks too thin, you have three levers: fewer guests, a simpler venue, or a shorter event.

## Splitting the budget by category

The chart below shows one common way a mid-range seated corporate event might divide its money. It is an example of the shape, not a rule: your event could weight AV or entertainment much more.

Food and beverage usually takes the biggest slice, and the bar can swing it by a large amount. Venue and rentals come next. When the venue includes tables and chairs, your rental line shrinks. When the venue is a raw space or a tent, rentals grow. Keep a line for decor and signage, since people forget them, and a line for staffing, which includes servers, bartenders, security and a coordinator if you hire one.

## Costs that surprise first-time planners

Service charges and taxes added to catering quotes. Overtime when setup runs long. Delivery and pickup fees. Parking or valet. Venue minimums that jump on prime dates. Insurance certificates a venue requires. Coat check. Extra staff for a second room. Ask every vendor for an all-in estimate and read the fine print on what is billed separately. For rentals, delivery, setup and pickup are often quoted separately from items, so compare totals. ## How to build the budget

<ol class="flow">
<li><strong>Get the total approved.</strong> Know who signs off and the date they need the final number.</li>
<li><strong>List fixed costs.</strong> Venue, AV, entertainment, decor, permits.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate the per-head pot.</strong> Subtract fixed costs and divide by expected attendance.</li>
<li><strong>Collect three quotes per major vendor.</strong> Ask for an all-in figure with service charges and taxes.</li>
<li><strong>Hold a contingency.</strong> Set aside 10 to 15 percent and do not spend it early.</li>
<li><strong>Track actuals weekly.</strong> Update the sheet as invoices arrive.</li>
</ol>

## Chicago-area notes

Chicago costs vary by location and season. Downtown and River North venues may bring parking, security and loading dock fees that suburban spaces do not, while suburban venues may save on rentals but need more delivery distance. Winter dates, especially December, tend to cost more and book faster. Ask venues about weekday or off-peak pricing. Rentals are usually priced per item with separate delivery fees that depend on location, so give your ZIP code when you request a quote. A village or park district permit may be needed for outdoor events, so ask early and budget for it without guessing a fee.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much should a corporate event cost per person?

There is no single number. A simple reception can cost far less than a plated dinner with entertainment. Start with your total budget divided by expected attendance, then confirm with real quotes in your area.

### What percent of the budget should go to food and beverage?

Many planners put 40 to 50 percent into food and beverage. It can be higher at a dinner-focused event and lower when AV or entertainment is large.

### What costs do people forget in a corporate event budget?

Service charges, taxes, delivery fees, overtime, parking, insurance certificates, signage and coat check. Ask each vendor for an all-in estimate to find them.

### Should I budget by registered guests or attendees?

Budget on registered guests, since vendors usually bill on your guarantee. You can set the guarantee a bit below registration if past events show no-shows, but check your contract terms first.

### How do I reduce the cost per person without hurting the event?

Choose a weekday, simplify the menu, trim the program length, or use the venue's included furniture. Rent only the pieces your layout needs.

### How big a contingency should I include?

A common rule is 10 to 15 percent of the total. Keep it untouched until late in planning, then release it as vendors finalize.

### Is it cheaper to host a corporate event at the office?

Sometimes, because there is no venue fee, but you will pay for rentals, catering setup and cleanup. Compare the full total, including staff time.
