**Quick answer**

Plan a charity walk or 5K about six months out. Choose a safe course, contact the park district or city early about permits, set a registration and fundraising plan, and arrange water, first aid, restrooms and volunteers. At the venue you will need tents for registration and sponsors, tables for check-in and refreshments, and a clear start and finish. Always plan for weather.

</div>

A charity walk looks simple: people show up, walk and go home. Behind it are permits, a safe route, water, first aid, restrooms, parking, sponsors and a lot of volunteers. The event raises money through registration fees and pledges, but it only works if participants have a good experience from the parking lot to the finish line. Start with the course and the permit, because they decide almost everything else.

## Walk and 5K planning rules of thumb

<div class="stat-grid">
<div><strong>6 months</strong><span>Common lead time, longer for city streets</span></div>
<div><strong>1 volunteer</strong><span>Per 8 to 10 participants is a typical staffing target</span></div>
<div><strong>1 water stop</strong><span>Every mile or so on warm days</span></div>
<div><strong>3.1 miles</strong><span>Length of a 5K, a mile or two for family walks</span></div>
<div><strong>Rain plan</strong><span>Decide in advance who calls it and when</span></div>
<div><strong>1 hour</strong><span>Allow time for check-in before the start</span></div>
</div>

These are common guidelines, not safety rules. Follow instructions from your permitting authority, local police, medical advisors and insurer for staffing, signage and emergency plans.

## Course, date and permits

Pick a route that is safe, easy to explain and large enough for your crowd. Parks, forest preserves, lakefront paths and school campuses are common choices, and a loop that starts and ends at the same place simplifies logistics. Walk the course yourself and note turns, narrow spots and crossings.

Permits are the long-lead item. Parks, paths and streets are controlled by park districts, forest preserve districts, villages or the city, and each has its own rules, timelines and insurance requirements. Contact them early and ask what is needed for amplified sound, tents, food, signage and road closures. Our [Chicago Park District event permit guide](/blog/chicago-park-district-event-permit) explains how to begin. Do not guess about fees or deadlines; ask each authority.

## Registration, fundraising and sponsors

Set registration tiers, such as early bird, standard and family, and give participants an easy way to create personal fundraising pages. Share a clear goal and a story about where the money goes. Sponsors are crucial for covering costs, so create packages with visible benefits, such as banners, a tent spot and mentions. Our [event sponsorship packages guide](/blog/event-sponsorship-packages-guide) walks through tiers.

On the day, you will need a check-in plan, bib or wristband pickup, a table for late registration and a help desk. Read the [registration and check-in setup guide](/blog/event-registration-and-check-in-setup) for layouts that keep lines short.

## Safety, volunteers and the day-of layout

Safety plans cover first aid, a way to contact emergency services, water, shade, restrooms and a plan for heat or storms. Assign marshals at turns and crossings, and brief them on what to do. [Volunteer coordination](/blog/volunteer-coordination-for-nonprofit-events) covers recruiting and training.

For layout, think of zones: parking and drop-off, registration, the start and finish area, a sponsor and community area, food and drink, and a medical point. Tents mark each zone and give shade and rain cover. Tables hold check-in materials, refreshments and giveaways. A 10 by 10 tent works for a check-in or first aid station, while a larger tent suits the finish party. See [what size tent you need](/blog/what-size-tent-do-i-need).

Keep the finish area clear of the check-in area so the crowd doesn't jam. Place first aid and restrooms where people can find them easily.

This table gives rough starting counts for tents and tables. They are planning guidelines, so adjust for your site.

|---|---|---|---|

Add tables for water, snacks, merchandise and sponsor displays, and consider extra chairs near the finish for people who need to rest.

## Planning steps

<li><strong>Apply for permits.</strong> Contact the park district, forest preserve, village or city early.</li>
<li><strong>Set registration and goals.</strong> Create fundraising pages and sponsor packages.</li>
<li><strong>Plan safety.</strong> Water, first aid, restrooms, marshals and a weather plan.</li>
<li><strong>Recruit volunteers.</strong> Assign roles and train them before the day.</li>
<li><strong>Order rentals.</strong> Tents, tables, chairs, linens and trash stations.</li>
<li><strong>Run the day.</strong> Open check-in early and keep a clear communication line.</li>
</ol>

## Chicago-area notes

In the Chicago area, spring and fall are popular for walks, which means weekends fill early. Lakefront and park routes are scenic but windy, and summer can be hot and humid. Winter walks need a plan for ice and cold. Check with the park district, forest preserve district or village for permits, insurance and tent rules, and read the [Chicago tent permit guide](/blog/chicago-tent-permit) if you plan a large tent. Parking can be a major issue, so consider shuttle options and encourage transit. Street closures need coordination with local police, so start those conversations well ahead.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I plan a charity walk?

Pick a safe course and date, apply for permits, set registration and fundraising goals, plan safety, recruit volunteers and arrange tents, tables and supplies.

### How far in advance should I plan a 5K?

About six months is typical, and longer if you need street closures. Permits and insurance often take the longest.

### Do I need a permit for a charity walk?

Often yes, depending on the location. Parks, forest preserves, villages and the city each have rules. Contact the relevant authority early rather than assuming.

### How many volunteers do I need?

A common planning target is one volunteer per eight to ten participants, but the number depends on course complexity and permit requirements.

### What do I need to rent for a charity walk?

Typically tents for check-in and sponsors, tables, chairs, linens, trash stations and sometimes staging for announcements.

### How do I raise more money at a charity walk?

Encourage participants to create fundraising pages, offer team options, secure sponsors, and add a donation or merchandise table.

### What if it rains on the day of the walk?

Decide in advance who makes the call and how you will communicate. Tents give cover for check-in and the finish. Check your permit for cancellation rules.
